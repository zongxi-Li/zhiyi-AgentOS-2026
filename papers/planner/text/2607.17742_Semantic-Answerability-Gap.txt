                                                                                                      A BSTRACT

                                                         In retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), semantic relevance asks whether a
                                                         source matches a query in meaning, while answerability asks whether it con-
                                                         tains sufficient information to answer the query. A Semantic-Answerability Gap
                                                         (SAG) may arise in retrieval when a retriever can reach semantically relevant
                                                         sources yet fail to identify those that are uniquely answerable. We uncover this
                                                         gap using tables as a controlled setting, where shared schemas and entities provide
                                                         strong semantic signals while localized content and row-column bindings distin-
                                                         guish answerable from non-answerable sources. Using TCR-Bench, a controlled
                                                         sibling-table benchmark, we find that dense retrievers achieve only 18.2% top-1
                                                         target retrieval, reducing QA F1 from 0.755 with the oracle table to 0.330 with
                                                         retrieved top-5 tables. Controlled diagnostics show that retrievers favor semantic
                                                         volume over sufficiency, respond weakly to row-column binding disruptions, and
                                                         struggle to distinguish Targets from Siblings. Explicit answerability assessment
                                                         substantially improves target identification, while fine-tuning shows that answer-
                                                         ability is learnable but difficult to transfer without compromising broad semantic
                                                         retrieval.


                                         1       I NTRODUCTION

                                         In retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems (Lewis et al., 2020; Huang & Huang, 2024), re-
                                         trieval must do more than find sources relevant to a query: the retrieved source should contain
                                         sufficient information to support a valid answer. We refer to this property as answerability, which
                                         is distinct from semantic relevance, or whether a source matches the meaning or topic of a query. A
                                         source may therefore be relevant without being sufficient to answer the query. This raises a natural
                                         question: can retrieval identify sources that are both relevant and answerable?

We study this question using tables as a controlled setting. Their structured, localized information
allows us to construct sources that are highly similar in semantics and structure but differ in whether
they contain the information needed to answer a query. This makes tables a useful setting for dis-
entangling semantic relevance from answerability. Moreover, tables are widely used as information
sources in enterprise databases, data lakes, and web resources (Cafarella et al., 2008; Stonebraker
et al., 2013; Nargesian et al., 2018), where semantically similar tables may likewise differ in the
information they contain.
Existing table QA benchmarks such as WikiTableQuestions and HybridQA (Pasupat & Liang, 2015;
Chen et al., 2020) assume that the relevant table is already given, and thus do not evaluate this
retrieval problem. Recent Table RAG work (Pan et al., 2022; Balaka et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025a)
improves retrieval and reasoning, yet existing evaluations largely emphasize semantic relevance
rather than whether a retrieved table is uniquely sufficient to answer the query. This makes it difficult
to isolate answerability from coarse semantic matching.
To study this distinction, we introduce TCR-Bench, a controlled diagnostic benchmark for Table
Content-level Answerability in RAG. TCR-Bench pairs answerable Target Tables with highly simi-
lar but non-answerable Sibling Distractors, varying answerability while keeping much semantic and
structural context fixed. This controlled design tests whether a retriever can identify the uniquely
answerable target rather than merely reaching its semantic neighborhood.
Using TCR-Bench, we identify a consistent Semantic-Answerability Gap (SAG) in retrieval: se-
mantically relevant sources can be reached without reliably identifying the uniquely answerable
one. We primarily study this phenomenon in dense retrieval. The best-performing dense retriever
reaches the relevant table group at top-1 for 67.0% of queries but retrieves the uniquely answerable
target for only 18.2%. This gap also has a clear downstream impact: QA F1 drops from 0.755
with the oracle table to 0.330 with retrieved top-5 tables. Diagnostics show that surface form is not
the main cause: retrievers favor semantic volume over sufficiency, respond weakly to column-value
binding disruptions, and form compact Target–Sibling neighborhoods without global collapse.
We further use Answerability-Aware Reranking (AAR) to test whether explicit query-conditioned
answerability assessment can alleviate this failure. AAR improves top-1 retrieval from 18.2% to
57.4%. However, answerability-oriented fine-tuning is strongly distribution-dependent, generic re-
trieval training does not induce comparable answerability discrimination, and simple joint training
does not recover the separate gains of the two objectives. Thus, learning answerability and preserv-
ing broad, transferable semantic retrieval remain distinct challenges.
Figure 2 summarizes the overall study. Our contributions are threefold. (1) We identify the
Semantic-Answerability Gap: we show that dense retrievers can reach the correct semantic neigh-
borhood while failing to identify the uniquely answerable target, establishing answerability as a
distinct retrieval dimension beyond semantic relevance. (2) We diagnose dense retrieval behav-
ior in a controlled setting: using TCR-Bench, we show that retrievers favor semantic volume
over sufficiency, remain weakly sensitive to disrupted row-column bindings, and place Targets and
Siblings in compact local neighborhoods without evidence of global collapse. (3) We investi-
gate answerability-aware retrieval: explicit answerability modeling substantially improves target
identification, while answerability-oriented, generic retrieval, and joint training experiments show
that transferable answerability discrimination and broad semantic retrieval do not necessarily align
across settings.1

More importantly, semantic similarity does not always imply answer support. Recent bench-
marks show that similarity-based retrieval aligns more with topical relevance than decision useful-
ness (Schimanski et al., 2026), while answer-centric supervision improves retrieval when positives
are defined by answer support (Nian et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2026). Other studies address mis-
leading context (Kim & Lee, 2024; Liu et al., 2026) and multi-hop retrieval (Trivedi et al., 2023;
Asai et al., 2024). However, these works do not isolate answerability as a distinct dimension from
semantic relevance or systematically disentangle the two under controlled conditions. We formalize
this mismatch as the SAG and study it through controlled sibling-table diagnostics.


2.2       TABLE RAG M ETHODS AND B ENCHMARKS

Table retrieval has explored both table-specific dense retrievers (Herzig et al., 2021) and generic re-
trievers such as DPR (Karpukhin et al., 2020), with evidence that generic architectures can perform
comparably to specialized ones (Wang et al., 2022). These findings suggest that structural bias alone
may not fully capture fine-grained distinctions within tables. Meanwhile, existing evaluations have
largely focused on semantic relevance, often leaving finer-grained content distinctions less exam-
ined. Simpler schema-aware and title-aware representations (Kim et al., 2024; Khanna & Subedi,
2025) have also shown strong performance, while hybrid and multi-table pipelines (Chen et al.,
2024b; Zhang et al., 2026) similarly focus on relevance matching. Taken together, these settings
often rely on relatively coarse signals of relevance, leaving finer-grained content-level distinctions
less directly tested.
Existing benchmarks (Herzig et al., 2021; Zhang & Balog, 2018; Cui et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2024;
Strich et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2026) increase difficulty through multi-hop reasoning,
mixed modalities, or heterogeneous corpora, yet consistently frame retrieval as semantic matching.
To our knowledge, existing benchmarks do not explicitly require distinguishing a uniquely answer-
able table from semantically similar candidates. TCR-Bench targets this gap directly through sibling
tables, isolating fine-grained answerability selection from coarse semantic retrieval.

3.1       A NSWERABILITY AS A D ISTINCT R ETRIEVAL D IMENSION

to a given query. This is distinct from semantic relevance: a table may be highly relevant to a query
while still being unable to answer it. We therefore distinguish three table types: Target Tables,
which are answerable; Sibling Distractor Tables, which are highly similar to the target but non-
answerable; and Non-Sibling Tables, which are less related in schema or semantics.

3.2              TCR-B ENCH : C ONTROLLED S IBLING -TABLE S ETTING

TCR-Bench is designed to diagnose the discrepancy between semantic relevance and answerability.
For each query, we construct one Target Table and multiple Sibling Distractor Tables from the same
source table. Sibling Distractor Tables preserve substantial semantic and structural similarity with
the target while being modified to become non-answerable. This creates a controlled setting where
semantic similarity is largely preserved while answerability varies.
We further vary queries, schemas, and table representations to reduce reliance on superficial match-
ing. Tables are rendered in Markdown, HTML, CSV, and Mixed formats. Detailed construction and
validation procedures are provided in Appendix A.

3.3              B ENCHMARK C ONSTRUCTION AND VALIDATION

TCR-Bench is constructed from databases originating from Spider (Yu et al., 2018) and BIRD (Li
et al., 2024). We formulate three TableQA tasks with increasing complexity: Exact Match (EM),
Selective Filtering (SF), and Selective Aggregation (SA), involving 1–3 conjunctive conditions.
Formal definitions are provided in Appendix A.2.
For each query, Sibling Distractor Tables are generated through controlled schema- and row-level
modifications, followed by answerability verification and manual validation. We additionally define
a diagnostic subset of EM queries with tables restricted to their sibling groups, allowing answer-
ability to be examined under largely fixed semantic similarity. The final benchmark contains 209
queries (98 EM, 55 SF, and 56 SA) and 637 tables.
To test whether the observed phenomenon extends beyond controlled construction, we additionally
introduce Real-TCR, a natural-negative collection derived from naturally occurring tables with real-
world data partitions. Unlike TCR-Bench, its candidates are not generated through answerability-
targeted transformations. Real-TCR contains 77 queries and 217 tables, while Real-TCR-small
contains 38 queries and 117 tables. Detailed construction and results are provided in Appendix E.

4.1.3    M AIN R ESULTS

Figure 3 reports retrieval performance under the Mixed format, with full results in Appendix D.1.
The results reveal a consistent Semantic-Answerability Gap (SAG) across the evaluated embed-
ding models. The strongest dense retriever achieves GR@1 = 0.670 but only R@1 = 0.182,
showing that identifying the correct semantic neighborhood is substantially easier than identifying
the answerable target. Scaling Qwen3 from 0.6B to 8B improves R@5 by 19.8%, but yields only
modest gains in DS@1.
Most models remain near or below the random-selection baseline of DS@1 = 0.298, with sig-
nificance analysis in Appendix M confirming that they do not significantly outperform chance.
Column-deletion cases provide a useful contrast: their R@1 drops to 0.072 for Qwen3-E4B and
0.057 for Stella, indicating sensitivity to explicit structural mismatches, while discrimination among
schema-consistent tables remains difficult. These results suggest that dense retrieval captures topi-
cal and schema-level similarity effectively, but is substantially less sensitive to fine-grained content
relations such as row-column bindings, value constraints, and condition consistency.
A consistent qualitative pattern emerges on Real-TCR. Jina achieves GR@1 = 0.948 but only
R@1 = 0.143 and DS@1 = 0.151, while Qwen3-E4B reaches GR@1 = 0.909 with DS@1 =
0.043. Across both Real-TCR and Real-TCR-small, the evaluated retrievers consistently achieve
high group recall but substantially lower target recall, indicating that the difficulty of distinguish-
ing the uniquely answerable target persists beyond the controlled setting of the main benchmark.
Complete results are reported in Appendix E.
More broadly, supplementary experiments with alternative first-stage retrievers, including DTR-
style, ColBERT-style, sparse, and hybrid methods, show that the observed gap is not substantially
reduced by these alternatives (Appendix D.2 and D.3).

4.3     T HE A NSWERABILITY B OTTLENECK

The main experiments establish two observations. First, retrievers reliably reach the correct semantic
group but rarely identify the answerable target. Second, downstream QA works well only when the
target is ranked first, while deeper retrieval mostly adds semantically similar but non-answerable
tables that interfere with generation.

    The Answerability Bottleneck
    Dense retrievers identify semantically relevant sources well but often perform close to the random-
    selection baseline when distinguishing the answerable target within that group. This SAG persists across
    the models and scales we evaluate, including different retrieval settings.



5     D IAGNOSING D ENSE R ETRIEVAL B EHAVIOR

The main experiments show that dense retrieval can often reach the semantic neighborhood contain-
ing the Target Table, but has difficulty distinguishing it from closely related Sibling Distractors. We
conduct controlled diagnostics to identify what information the evaluated representations rely on
and where this discrimination failure arises. We first examine (1) sensitivity to surface-level serial-
ization and query wording, and then investigate (2) sensitivity to semantic content and fine-grained
table structure, including the embedding geometry underlying the observed behavior.

5.1     ROBUSTNESS TO S URFACE VARIATIONS

We first test whether the observed behavior depends on how tables or queries are expressed. For
tables, we evaluate six serialization formats on the Diagnostic Subset: CSV, HTML, Markdown,
Mixed, and two sentence-based representations, TSen and TSenS. TSen converts each row into
a declarative sentence while preserving attribute order, whereas TSenS randomly shuffles the at-
tributes. For queries, we compare three semantically equivalent formulations: the original natural-
language query, a sentential formulation, and a template-based formulation (Appendix F.1).
Across these variations, retrieval remains broadly stable for both Qwen3-E4B and Stella. Qwen3-
E4B achieves R@1 between 0.186 and 0.257 across the six table formats, with GR@1 between
0.757 and 0.857. Query reformulation similarly produces limited changes. Thus, the Target–Sibling
discrimination failure is not primarily an artifact of serialization or query wording. Complete results
and consistency analyses are provided in Appendix F.

6     M ODELING A NSWERABILITY FOR R ETRIEVAL

To further examine whether explicitly modeling answerability can alleviate the SAG, we conduct
two complementary experiments: answerability-aware reranking and answerability-oriented fine-
tuning. The former asks whether query-conditioned interaction can distinguish the target from se-
mantically similar tables, while the latter asks whether this behavior can be learned by an embedding
model and transferred beyond the training distribution.


Query-Conditioned Answerability Assessment. We use Answerability-Aware Reranking
(AAR) as a diagnostic framework to explicitly assess answerability after dense retrieval. Unlike
single-vector retrieval, query-conditioned interaction directly evaluates whether a candidate table
is sufficient to satisfy the query. We consider three variants: AAR-CE, using Qwen3-Reranker-
8B (Qwen3-R8B) paired with the Qwen3-E8B retriever; AAR-LLM-PW, using Qwen3-30B as a
pointwise LLM-based answerability classifier (Balaka et al., 2025); and AAR-LLM-LW, using the
same Qwen3-30B model to jointly rank candidates in a listwise setting.


            Table 4: Answerability-aware reranking on the Qwen3-E8B backbone (k=10).
                             Method              R@1         R@5     GR@1     DS@1
                             AAR-CE              0.574       0.656   0.766     0.750
                             AAR-LLM-PW          0.536       0.737   0.761     0.704
                             AAR-LLM-LW          0.450       0.708   0.770     0.584


All three variants substantially improve target identification over dense retrieval, with R@1 increas-
ing from 0.182 to 0.450–0.574. The gain persists under both pointwise and listwise LLM reranking,
indicating that it is not specific to a particular reranking formulation. The matched Qwen3-E8B
and Qwen3-R8B comparison further suggests that the improvement is associated with the change
from dense embedding to query-conditioned reranking rather than model scale: both use the same
pretrained initialization and 8B parameter scale, but differ in objective. Full results and significance
analysis are in Appendix K and Appendix M.


Learning Answerability in Embeddings. We further fine-tune Qwen3-E0.6B with query–target
positives and sibling-table hard negatives to test whether answerability can be learned directly by an
embedding model. The best TCR-Bench model improves R@1 from 0.182 to 0.493 and DS@1 from
0.290 to 0.691, showing that answerability is learnable under matched supervision. However, the
learned signal is strongly distribution-dependent: the TCR-Bench-trained model achieves only 0.065
R@1 on Real-TCR and substantially degrades retrieval on the Table RAG benchmark TARGET (Ji
et al., 2024). The reverse transfer from Real-TCR to TCR-Bench is similarly weak, while generic
training on TabFact and FeTaQA, two subsets of TARGET, improves semantic retrieval without
inducing comparable answerability-sensitive discrimination. Moreover, a simple mixture of TCR-
Bench and TARGET training data does not simultaneously recover the gains of the two objectives.
Thus, learning answerability and preserving broad, transferable semantic retrieval remain distinct
challenges. Detailed configurations and results are given in Appendix L.

7   C ONCLUSION
Our experiments reveal a consistent Semantic-Answerability Gap (SAG) in retrieval: semantically
relevant sources can be reached without reliably identifying the uniquely answerable target. We
primarily study this phenomenon in dense retrieval, using TCR-Bench to isolate the gap through
sibling tables that are highly similar in semantics and structure but differ in answerability. Our diag-
nostics show that retrievers favor semantic volume over sufficiency, respond weakly to row-column
bindings, and leave Targets and Siblings hard to separate locally. Explicit answerability model-
ing substantially improves target identification, while fine-tuning shows that answerability can be
learned but remains strongly distribution-dependent. Generic retrieval training does not automati-
cally induce comparable answerability-sensitive discrimination, and simple joint training does not
simultaneously preserve gains in answerability and broad semantic retrieval. These findings moti-
vate retrieval objectives and models that treat answerability as a distinct retrieval dimension while
jointly preserving broad semantic relevance and transferable answerability discrimination.

data or identifying information from this collection. Practitioners applying Table RAG methods to

safeguards beyond those provided by this benchmark.

A     B ENCHMARK C ONSTRUCTION D ETAILS
This section provides the detailed construction procedures and implementation constraints used to
instantiate TCR-Bench. The goal is to isolate content-level answerability from coarse semantic rel-
evance: for each query, an answerable Target Table is paired with highly similar but non-answerable
Sibling Distractor Tables, so that answerability can vary while much of the semantic and structural
context remains fixed. Here we describe how these tables are generated, how surface-level shortcuts
are controlled, and how unique answerability and annotation quality are verified.

A.1   S OURCE TABLES AND Q UERY G ENERATION

We construct TCR-Bench from databases originating from Spider (Yu et al., 2018) and BIRD (Li
et al., 2024). These benchmarks provide relational tables from diverse real-world domains and

Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




naturally occurring schemas, allowing our controlled construction to retain realistic table structures
while enabling precise manipulation of answer-bearing evidence.
For each source table, we generate TableQA queries based on its schema and cell values. We con-
sider three task types: Exact Match (EM), Selective Filtering (SF), and Selective Aggregation (SA),
corresponding to progressively more complex query semantics. Each query contains 1–3 conjunc-
tive conditions over target columns. The formal definitions and examples of these tasks are provided
in Appendix A.2.
For each query, the original source table serves as the basis for constructing a Target Table and
its associated Sibling Distractor Tables. The Target Table remains uniquely answerable, whereas
Sibling Distractors are produced by controlled schema- and row-level modifications that preserve
substantial semantic and structural similarity while removing the evidence needed to answer the
query.

A.2   TASK D EFINITIONS

We formalize three query-answer tasks of increasing semantic complexity: Exact Match (EM), Se-
lective Filtering (SF), and Selective Aggregation (SA). Each task is defined over a relational table T
with schema S = (A1 , . . . , Ak ), where each attribute Ai takes values from a discrete or continuous
domain.
A query Q comprises a set of selection predicates P and, depending on the task type, may addition-
ally specify a target attribute At and an aggregation function f . Let R ⊆ T denote the set of rows
satisfying all predicates in P:

                                  R = { r ∈ T | ∀p ∈ P : p(r) = true }.

Exact Match (EM). In the EM setting, every predicate in P is a conjunction of equality conditions
of the form Ai = vi , where vi is a literal value from the domain of Ai . The query requests the
value(s) of a designated target column At from the rows in R. Under the key-based uniqueness
assumption, R is either empty or a singleton. In practice, the answer is the unique value of At in the
matching row.
Example. For the table shown in Table 5, the query “What is C when A = 5 and B = 3?” yields the
unique answer 4.

Selective Filtering (SF). The SF task relaxes the equality restriction. At least one predicate em-
ploys a comparison operator from the set <, >, ≤, ≥, ̸=. Predicates may still contain equality con-
ditions, but the filter is no longer restricted to exact key lookups. The answer is the set of values of
the target column At over the filtered rows R.
Example. The query “What is C when A = 5 and B ¡ 5?” filters the rows using an equality condition
on A and an inequality condition on B, returning 4.

Selective Aggregation (SA). The SA task extends SF with a downstream aggregation operation.
The query first applies the selection predicates P to obtain the filtered set R, and then computes an
aggregate statistic over the values of a target column At . The aggregation function f is drawn from
SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX.
The answer is the scalar result

                                          f ({ r[At ] | r ∈ R }).

Example. The query “What is the average of C when A ≥ 5 and B ≥ 3?” filters the rows satisfying
both inequalities and returns the mean of the corresponding C values, which is 5.
These three tasks form a progression from exact symbolic lookup to conditional filtering and aggre-
gation over a set of retrieved values. Together, they probe complementary reasoning requirements
for determining whether a table contains sufficient evidence to answer a query.

A.3     S IBLING D ISTRACTOR G ENERATION

The primary objective of Sibling Distractor construction is to preserve semantic and structural sim-
ilarity while removing answer-bearing evidence. We therefore generate distractors from the same
source table rather than independently sampling unrelated tables.
We employ three complementary generation strategies:

         • Removing Condition or Target Columns. We remove either a query-condition column
           or the target column from the source table. Only one column is removed at a time, so
           that the resulting table retains most of the original schema while becoming insufficient for
           answering the query.
         • Row Deletion. We randomly delete 30%–70% of the rows from the source table. Addi-
           tional rows are subsequently sampled from the remaining corpus when necessary to satisfy
           the predefined table-length constraint. Rows that would provide the required answer evi-
           dence are explicitly excluded.
         • Row Resampling. We construct a new table by resampling rows from the available table
           corpus while preserving the relevant schema characteristics. The resulting table remains
           structurally plausible but does not contain the evidence required by the query.

These strategies create distractors with different forms of missing evidence. Column-level modifica-
tions primarily remove schema or attribute grounding, whereas row-level modifications remove the
specific records required to satisfy the query. Combining these mechanisms prevents the benchmark
from reducing answerability to a single perturbation pattern.

A.4     S AMPLING C ONSTRAINTS AND A NSWER F ILTERING

To ensure comparable retrieval conditions across candidate tables, all generated sub-tables are con-
strained to approximately 6,500–7,800 tokens, measured using tiktoken2 . This relatively large
context size is intended to embed the relevant evidence within a substantial amount of table con-
tent, so that retrieval cannot be trivially solved by inspecting a small table. As we further show in
Appendix D.5, however, this challenge persists even for smaller tables.
For row deletion and row resampling, we explicitly filter out rows that would allow the query to be
answered correctly. More importantly, answerability is verified globally across the complete bench-
mark corpus rather than only within the corresponding sibling group. This prevents an independently
sampled table from accidentally becoming an alternative answerable table for the same query.
After this filtering procedure, each query is associated with exactly one valid Target Table. Sib-
ling Distractor Tables remain semantically plausible but fail at least one requirement necessary for
producing a valid answer.

A.5     Q UERY AND S CHEMA AUGMENTATION

We introduce controlled semantic and surface variations to reduce the possibility that retrieval can
be solved through direct lexical matching. These transformations are applied while preserving the
underlying query semantics and answerability relation. Tables are additionally rendered in Mark-
down, HTML, CSV, and Mixed formats. The Mixed format refers to a combination of the first three,
where each table is randomly assigned one of them; this assignment is pre-determined to ensure
experimental consistency.

A.5.1    Q UERY AND S CHEMA PARAPHRASING

Queries and schemas are paraphrased to introduce linguistic and naming variation. The generated
paraphrases are required to preserve the original query intent, constraints, and target attributes. Man-
ual validation is subsequently applied to cases where the generated text may introduce semantic
shifts.
Schema paraphrasing is particularly important because Sibling Distractor Tables originate from the
same source table and therefore naturally share substantial lexical overlap. Varying column names
reduces the extent to which retrieval can rely on direct schema-token matching.

A.5.2    DATE F ORMAT AUGMENTATION

Date values are transformed into multiple surface representations while preserving their underlying
temporal values. Specifically, standard Y Y Y Y − M M − DD representations may be converted
into:

        • English Abbreviations: e.g., 15/Jan/2023 or Jan. 15, 2023.
        • Full English Spellings: e.g., January 15th, 2023.
        • Roman Numerals: e.g., 2023-I-15.
        • Numeric Words: e.g., two thousand twenty-three. one. fifteen.

These transformations preserve the semantic identity of dates while substantially changing their
token-level representations.

A.5.3    N UMERIC AND S CALE P ERTURBATIONS

We similarly transform numerical values to reduce reliance on exact lexical matching. The augmen-
tation strategies include:

        • Scaling Large Numbers: numbers in the thousands are represented using compact forms
          with k or K, e.g., 15000 → 15k.
        • Multiplicative and Additive Shifts: numerical values are perturbed through controlled
          multiplication, division, or small additive shifts. The type of perturbation applied to each
          column is explicitly indicated in the column name and also communicated to the model in
          the relevant prompt. This type of perturbation is applied relatively infrequently.
        • Numeral-System Conversion: numbers are represented using Roman numerals or English
          words, e.g., 102 → C or One hundred and two.

All transformations are applied subject to the requirement that they do not alter the intended query
semantics or introduce unintended answerable distractors.

A.6     S EMANTIC AUGMENTATION M ODEL

We use GPT-OSS-120B (OpenAI, 2025), an open-weight model released by OpenAI under the
Apache 2.0 license, for query and schema paraphrasing. At the time of benchmark construction, it
provided a strong locally deployable open-weight option, allowing the augmentation process to be
reproduced without reliance on proprietary API endpoints.
The model is used only for semantic augmentation and does not participate in retrieval or down-
stream question answering. To reduce the possibility that benchmark behavior is tied to the augmen-
tation model, subsequent experimental components, including dense retrievers, rerankers, and QA
models, are selected from non-GPT model families.
All generated paraphrases are subsequently validated to ensure that they preserve the original query
intent and schema semantics.

A.7   M ANUAL VALIDATION AND FALSE -N EGATIVE R EDUCTION

We employ a multi-stage validation procedure to improve annotation quality and reduce false-
negative cases.
First, answerability is verified programmatically across all tables. Within each sibling group, only
the Target Table is allowed to satisfy all query constraints. We additionally evaluate the query logic
against the full candidate corpus to identify tables outside the sibling group that may inadvertently
contain sufficient evidence to answer the query.
Second, we conduct targeted manual inspection of potentially ambiguous tables. For each query, we
retrieve the top-k candidate tables outside its corresponding sibling group using multiple similarity
signals. Candidate sets from different retrieval signals are merged, and the resulting tables are
manually examined for semantic equivalence with the Target Table. This procedure focuses on cases
where different schemas or column names may express the same underlying concept and therefore
create unintended alternative answer tables. Ambiguous cases are removed from the benchmark.
Third, all generated query and schema transformations are manually checked. Annotators verify
that rewritten queries preserve the original intent and constraints, and that schema transformations
remain semantically consistent with the source tables.
Approximately 5% of GPT-OSS-generated paraphrases required manual correction due to semantic
shifts.
Although complete elimination of false negatives is difficult in an open-ended semantic space, the
combination of global answerability checking, targeted retrieval-based inspection, and manual vali-
dation substantially reduces this risk.

A.8   B ENCHMARK S TATISTICS

The final benchmark contains 209 queries, including 98 EM, 55 SF, and 56 SA queries, together
with 637 tables. Tables may be shared across different query instances. We additionally define a
diagnostic subset restricted to sibling groups, allowing answerability to be examined under largely
fixed semantic similarity.
The benchmark is intentionally moderate in corpus size because its primary purpose is controlled
diagnosis rather than large-scale corpus retrieval. The difficulty is introduced through the construc-
tion of semantically similar Sibling Distractor Tables, unique answerability, large table contexts, and
surface-level variations.

B     D ETAILED D ESCRIPTION OF E MBEDDING M ODELS

This appendix summarizes the embedding model families evaluated in the main experiments. Since
many table-specific embedding models, like DTR (Herzig et al., 2021), often use very limited con-
text, and studies such as Wang et al. (Wang et al., 2022) have shown that table-specific embeddings
do not necessarily outperform general text embeddings in the Table RAG domain, we focus only on
commonly used, open-source, and relatively strong embedding models, all of which are capable of
handling 8K context windows.

       • Qwen3 Embedding Family (Zhang et al., 2025b). The Qwen3 embedding series includes
         Qwen3-E0.6B, Qwen3-E4B, and Qwen3-E8B. These instruction-aware text embedding
         models support long contexts (up to 32k tokens), multilingual input (100+ languages), and
         Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) for flexible embedding dimensions. Larger
         variants generally provide stronger semantic representation quality.
       • Stella (Zhang et al., 2025a). stella en 1.5B v5 is a 1.5B-parameter English sentence em-
         bedding model optimized for semantic similarity and dense retrieval. It produces fixed-
         length embeddings and serves as a competitive mid-scale dense baseline.
       • Jina (Günther et al., 2025). jina-embeddings-v4 is a ∼3.8B-parameter multilingual and
         multimodal embedding model. It supports unified text-image representations, as well as
         both single-vector dense and multi-vector late-interaction retrieval.

We further reproduce CRAFT (Singh et al., 2026), a multi-model cascaded retrieval framework that
combines sparse and dense retrieval with LLM-based text augmentation. To adapt CRAFT to our
setting, we replace its original retrieval backbones with available open-source checkpoints while pre-
serving the overall cascading procedure. Specifically, the retrieval cascade consists of splade-v3,
Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, and Qwen3-Embedding-4B. We evaluate two variants. CRAFT-Simplified
removes the LLM-based augmentation component and therefore consists solely of the retrieval cas-
cade. The full CRAFT variants additionally apply LLM-based augmentation using either Qwen3-
30B-A3B-2507-Thinking or Qwen3.6-27B. This setup allows us to separately assess the effective-
ness of the cascaded retrieval architecture and the LLM-based augmentation component under our
long-context table setting.
Finally, we reproduce THYME (Li et al., 2025a), a table-specific retrieval method that adopts a
column-oriented representation and combines multiple retrieval signals over table fields. THYME
is particularly relevant as a direct comparison with DTR: both are table-specific retrieval archi-
tectures trained specifically for table retrieval, rather than general-purpose retrievers applied to
tables. We evaluate THYME using bert-base-uncased, bert-large-uncased, and
splade cocondenser selfdistil, corresponding to the backbone configurations consid-
ered in the original work. We preserve its column-oriented retrieval paradigm as closely as possible
without additional training on our benchmark.


Results Table 9 summarizes the retrieval performance across paradigms. Overall, most alternative
retrieval paradigms underperform the strongest dense and row-level multi-vector baselines in our
setting. DTR performs particularly poorly, with both variants achieving R@1 below 1%. THYME
performs better, with its strongest configuration reaching 0.062 R@1 and 0.371 DS@1, but remains
substantially behind the strongest general-purpose retrieval methods.

highest group recall, exact retrieval, and downstream QA performance among the three types. Even

discrimination, obtaining the lowest R@1 and DS@1 of the three types. Third, coarse retrieval
and within-group discrimination behave as distinct sources of difficulty: SF has the lowest GR@1
among the three types, yet its DS@1 is higher than that of both EM and SA, showing that difficulty

that group once it has been located.
These results suggest that retrieval difficulty and reasoning difficulty are related but not interchange-
able. Given SA’s low retrieval performance and low Oracle score, the non-monotonic Eff@k pattern
for this type should be interpreted with caution: SA has comparatively few queries, so the effective
sample after retrieval is small and downstream QA estimates can be unstable. We therefore refrain
from drawing strong conclusions from minor numeric variations in Eff@k.

E           R EAL -TCR: NATURAL -N EGATIVE VALIDATION

TCR-Bench is intentionally constructed as a controlled stress test in which sibling tables share coarse
semantic and structural characteristics while differing in whether the evidence required by a query
is present. While this controlled construction makes answerability measurable and enables system-
atic analysis, it also raises a natural concern: whether the observed Semantic-Answerability Gap is
specific to the benchmark construction or also occurs in naturally organized table collections.
To address this concern, we construct Real-TCR, an additional evaluation collection from naturally
occurring tables in a real-world environment. Unlike TCR-Bench, its candidate tables are not gener-
ated through answerability-targeted transformations. Instead, they follow natural partitions already
present in the underlying data, such as by time window or organizational unit. As a result, the tables
exhibit naturally occurring variation in schema, column sets, and length. The data are substantially
different in both content and organization from the open-source data used in TCR-Bench, providing
an additional evaluation in a naturally organized setting.
Real-TCR contains 77 queries and 217 tables, while Real-TCR-small contains 38 queries and 117
tables, with candidate groups capped at 10 tables. Table lengths range from 3K to 8K tokens. The
corresponding random DS@1 baselines are 0.047 and 0.100, respectively.

G.1     S EMANTIC - VOLUME PERTURBATION

We investigate whether dense retrieval is sensitive to the amount of semantic content or to the in-
formation required for identification. For each table row, we construct three sentence-based repre-
sentations. Sen converts the complete row into a declarative sentence while preserving the original
attribute order. SenS applies the same conversion but randomly shuffles the attribute order within
the sentence. SenSS retains only a subset of column-value pairs that is sufficient to uniquely identify
the relevant row.
For illustration, given a row containing the attributes Year, Country, Product, and Sales, the repre-
sentations can be written as follows:

         Sen: In 2020, the country was France, the product was Wine, and sales were 120.
         SenS: The product was Wine, sales were 120, in 2020, and the country was
         France.
         SenSS: In 2020, the product was Wine and sales were 120.

Sen and SenS preserve the complete row, whereas SenSS retains only a subset sufficient for identifi-
cation in the corresponding diagnostic case. SenS isolates sensitivity to attribute order, while SenSS
examines whether retrieval benefits from additional semantic content beyond what is logically suf-
ficient for identification.
We evaluate these representations at both the inter-table and intra-table levels. At the inter-table
level, the task is to identify the Target Table from the candidate tables. At the intra-table level, the
task is to retrieve the relevant row or a chunk containing it from the Target Table.
The results show that Sen and SenS generally outperform SenSS despite SenSS retaining sufficient
information for identification, while the difference between Sen and SenS remains small. This
indicates that the evaluated embeddings are more responsive to the amount of semantic content than
to attribute order or minimal information sufficiency.
At the intra-table level, row-level retrieval substantially outperforms retrieval over 10-line chunks.
Figure 4 presents the corresponding results. This contrast indicates that the evaluated dense em-
beddings can capture local row-level content while suffering from information dilution at larger
granularities.

Where the failure sits. Neighborhood shape alone does not strongly predict Target ranking: local
deff and local d¯ correlate only weakly with R@1 (|r| ≲ 0.25). The query-conditioned margin ∆q
is more closely aligned with this within-neighborhood scoring step: it correlates with R@1 at about
0.32–0.54, but barely with GR@1. On average ∆q is negative for every model we evaluate, and
only 21%–29% of queries score the Target above the nearest Sibling. When the retriever reaches the
neighborhood and also picks the Target, mean ∆q is positive (e.g., +0.025 for Qwen3-Embedding-
4B Mixed); when it reaches the neighborhood but picks a Sibling, mean ∆q is negative (−0.041 on
the same setting). We therefore treat ∆q as a direct diagnostic of query-to-table scoring inside the
sibling group, rather than as evidence for a new causal mechanism.

Scope of the local diagnosis. The local quantities above are defined on Target+Sibling Distractor
groups. This controlled structure makes it possible to separate two retrieval stages that coincide
under a singleton gold set: reaching a semantic neighborhood versus selecting the answerable Tar-

tics enabled by the controlled retrieval structure, rather than as generic properties of table-retrieval
benchmarks.

Takeaway. Dense encoders do not simply collapse table representations into an undifferentiated
space. Related tables instead form coherent local neighborhoods, as reflected by the relatively small
local cosine distances together with substantial GR@1. The remaining difficulty is concentrated
within those neighborhoods: distinguishing the answerable Target from Sibling Distractors. The
Target–Sibling margin ∆q is more directly related to this within-neighborhood scoring step than
aggregate geometric statistics, providing a geometric characterization of the retrieval behavior un-
derlying the observed GR@1–R@1 discrepancy.
Answerability-oriented fine-tuning on TCR-Bench (Appendix L) changes this picture in a way that is
consistent with the same diagnostic. After tuning, mean ∆q becomes positive and the neighborhood-
hit/Target-miss rate drops, so the encoder does learn to score the Target above nearby Siblings on
the training groups. At the same time local d¯ rises sharply (median 0.113 to 0.481 for Qwen3-
Embedding-0.6B Mixed): the compact sibling pockets of the base encoder are no longer preserved.
The geometry therefore supports both sides of the training appendix—answerability is learnable
inside matched sibling groups, but the update is not a drop-in repair of generic semantic retrieval.

are compact before tuning spread apart afterward, and the mass of ∆q moves from below zero to
above zero.

This experiment provides an exploratory analysis of how the evaluated embeddings respond to
schema-level and cell-level changes. We construct controlled minimal contrasts that isolate individ-
ual factors while keeping the remaining table content unchanged. The goal is to examine whether
retrieval similarity responds differently to structural signals and factual cell content, providing a
complementary perspective on the schema-level cue dependence observed on TCR-Bench.

H.1   E XPERIMENTAL D ESIGN

“Price” → “Column 2”) without modifying cell values; and (3) Schema Perturbation (Column
Deletion), which removes the column referenced by the query to examine the effect of removing a
relevant structural dimension.

These perturbations are deliberately small and isolate individual factors as cleanly as possible. This
design differs from the construction of TCR-Bench, which uses a broader set of transformations and
substantially larger contrasts between target and sibling tables. In particular, the perturbations in
this appendix are minimal contrasts: only a small number of cells or a single schema component
is modified while the remainder of the table is kept unchanged. The cell-level perturbation here di-
rectly replaces answer-relevant values with other entries from the same column, which can therefore
change the factual identity of the affected entities. In contrast, the cell-level transformations used
in TCR-Bench are designed to preserve the semantic identity of the underlying entities, for exam-
ple through semantically similar substitutions, while introducing substantially larger changes in the
table content. These differences make the two settings complementary: the present experiment iso-
lates the effect of small, single-factor changes, whereas TCR-Bench evaluates retrieval under more
substantial content and structural differences.
The difference is also reflected in the degree of overlap between targets and sibling tables. The
minimal contrasts used here are intentionally much more similar than typical target-sibling pairs in
TCR-Bench. Even when rows are shared in TCR-Bench, the maximum row-level overlap is approx-
imately 70%, and such highly overlapping cases constitute only a small fraction of the benchmark.
Moreover, TCR-Bench incorporates multiple transformation strategies rather than selecting a sin-
gle dimension according to its effect on retrieval. The present experiment therefore serves as a
controlled diagnostic analysis rather than a reproduction of the benchmark construction process.

H.2             R ESULTS AND A NALYSIS

Table 26 reports retrieval performance under these perturbations on the Mixed format. The results
suggest an asymmetry in how the evaluated retrievers respond to schema-level and cell-level mod-
ifications. For example, for Qwen3-E4B, DS@1 is 0.578 under content perturbation, compared
with 0.810 when a query-relevant header is renamed. Since a higher DS@1 indicates a stronger
preference for the original target over its perturbed counterpart, the retriever is substantially more
responsive to the header modification than to the corresponding cell-level modification in this con-
trolled setting. A similar pattern is observed for Stella, where DS@1 increases from 0.611 under
content perturbation to 0.771 under header renaming.
The cell-level perturbation results indicate that changing factual values alone can have a relatively
limited effect on retrieval similarity when the surrounding schema remains unchanged. Although


                                                                                               36
Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




the perturbed tables no longer satisfy the original query conditions, their unchanged headers and
structural organization can preserve substantial similarity to the target. This behavior is consistent
with the failure mode studied in TCR-Bench, where semantically similar tables can remain difficult
to distinguish when answerability depends on fine-grained factual content. However, because the
perturbations here are intentionally much smaller than those in TCR-Bench, these results should
not be interpreted as estimating the prevalence or severity of this failure mode in realistic table
collections.
Column deletion provides an additional structural contrast. For Qwen3-E4B, deleting the query-
relevant column produces a DS@1 of 0.738, while Stella obtains 0.629. These values exceed the
corresponding sensitivity to content perturbation for both models, although the magnitude of the
difference varies across models. Together with the header-renaming results, these observations sug-
gest that the evaluated embedding models can be substantially affected by changes to structural or
schema-level signals, even when factual cell values remain unchanged.


H.3   I NTERPRETATION AND L IMITATIONS

The results provide preliminary evidence that the evaluated embedding representations may rely
more strongly on schema-level signals than on fine-grained factual cell-level signals in minimal
contrastive settings. This may explain why tables with highly similar schemas but different answer-
bearing values can remain difficult to distinguish. However, we treat this observation as exploratory
rather than as a definitive characterization of the underlying representations.
A key consideration is that schema tokens typically occur near the beginning of serialized tables.
Consequently, modifying a header also changes information at a highly salient positional region,
making positional sensitivity a potential confounding factor. Section I holds table content and an-
swerability fixed and varies only the position of the relevant row; that analysis shows that front-
loaded content receives a measurable retrieval advantage. The larger response to header perturba-
tions therefore cannot be attributed solely to schema semantics.
This positional confound primarily affects the interpretation of why the retrieval behavior occurs,
rather than the empirical observation itself. Regardless of whether the underlying cause is schema
semantics, positional salience, or their interaction, modifying relevant schema information can sub-
stantially alter retrieval behavior, whereas small factual changes can sometimes leave retrieval simi-
larity largely unchanged. The present experiment therefore identifies a retrieval asymmetry without
attempting to isolate its underlying causal mechanism.
Overall, this analysis serves as a controlled manipulation check that complements the broader bench-
mark results. It uses minimal single-factor contrasts to characterize model sensitivity, whereas TCR-
Bench evaluates retrieval under more diverse and substantially larger content and structural transfor-
mations. The two settings consequently answer different questions: this experiment examines how
retrieval responds to isolated changes, while TCR-Bench evaluates whether the tested retrievers can
distinguish an answerable Target from semantically similar but non-answerable Sibling Distractors.
The observed asymmetry is one possible explanation for that failure mode. The following section
tests the positional confound; the precise contributions of schema information, positional effects,
and cell-level representations remain open.

I     P OSITION B IAS AND THE “H EADER P REFERENCE ”
The header-renaming contrast in Section H cannot separate schema semantics from positional
salience, because headers occupy the beginning of a serialized table. We therefore test whether
the evaluated embeddings exhibit position bias when table content and answerability are held fixed.
Prior work on long-context language models has shown that performance can vary with the location
of relevant information, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the lost-in-the-middle effect (Liu
et al., 2024a). Here we ask a narrower question: whether the physical placement of a relevant row
affects retrieval similarity even when both tables remain fully answerable.

I.1    E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

We construct pairs of tables that are semantically identical and both answerable. The only differ-
ence is the position of the relevant row. The row is placed in one of three positions:

        • Top: immediately after the header
        • Middle: within the body of the table
        • Bottom: near the end of the table

We evaluate four comparison settings:

        • Top vs Original (original row order in the dataset)
        • Top vs Middle
        • Bottom vs Middle
        • Top vs Bottom

For each comparison, the table listed first is treated as the formal Target for scoring, although both
tables are answerable.
We reuse DS@1 as a pairwise preference score. A value greater than 0.5 indicates that the model
tends to rank the first table higher, while a value below 0.5 indicates preference for the second table.
Thus, DS@1 here reflects positional preference rather than answerability discrimination.

I.3   D ISCUSSION

Our analysis indicates that the embedding models we evaluate exhibit a noticeable position bias
when encoding tables. This observation is consistent with prior studies on long-context language
models, which show that models often favor information appearing near the beginning or end of
the input sequence while struggling to utilize information located in the middle (Liu et al., 2024a).
Rows placed closer to the header are more likely to influence the embedding representation and thus
receive higher similarity scores during retrieval.
Notably, the preference for top-positioned rows persists even when the alternative placement is only
moved to the middle of the table, indicating that positional cues can influence similarity scores in this
setting. Similar positional effects have been reported in transformer-based models, where attention
patterns and positional encodings can introduce systematic biases toward certain regions of the input
sequence (Yu et al., 2025b).
The position effect is weaker than the header-renaming contrast in Section H, so positional salience
does not by itself account for the full schema-versus-cell asymmetry. It does, however, show that
front-loaded content receives an independent retrieval advantage. Combined with the previous sec-
tion, the header-renaming result should therefore be read as a mixture of schema-level and positional
effects, rather than as a pure test of schema semantics.

I.4   I MPLICATION

Because both tables in this experiment are answerable, the result does not measure answerability
discrimination. It shows that, even with answerability held fixed, the evaluated embeddings remain
sensitive to where relevant evidence appears. This positional preference is one reason header-level
modifications can move retrieval more than small factual edits in our contrasts, and it is consistent
with encoding tables more like linearized text than like an explicit record of row-column bindings.
Primacy effects of this kind have also been reported in long-context evaluation (Liu et al., 2024a;
Veseli et al., 2025).


J     R ETRIEVAL UNDER TABLE T RANSPOSITION

Prior studies in TableQA and table representation learning have shown that table orientation can
substantially affect model behavior, especially for architectures relying on serialized table represen-
tations and positional encoding schemes (Liu et al., 2024b; Sui et al., 2024; Singha et al., 2023).
Motivated by these observations, we investigate whether table transposition influences retrieval per-
formance in our setting.
Specifically, we transpose each table by swapping rows and columns while preserving all original
cell contents. The experiment is conducted exclusively under the Mixed format setting.

Experimental Setup We evaluate representative dense embeddings on the transposed-table corpus
using the same retrieval protocol and evaluation metrics as in Section 4. To test whether the observed
sensitivity is specific to single-vector retrieval, we additionally evaluate the row-level multi-vector
approach from Appendix D.2 under the same transposed-table setting. This method always embeds
each row of the table as presented: after transposition, a “row” is a row of the transposed table (i.e.,
an original column laid out as a row). We do not switch to column-wise indexing in order to recover
the original entity rows.

Analysis All three evaluated single-vector embeddings show substantial degradation after table
transposition.
A plausible account has two parts. First, the original tables are largely row-oriented: attributes of the
same entity remain locally grouped after serialization. Many queries in TCR-Bench depend on such
entity-level rows, so this layout can help the evaluated embeddings capture local row-level content.
After transposition, related attributes become dispersed, weakening that local grouping. This is
a stronger structural change than the format-level serialization variants considered in Section 5.1,
which left Target–Sibling discrimination largely stable.
Second, transposition scatters schema information across the sequence and disrupts the alignment
between values and their surrounding context. This is consistent with the header/top preference in
Section I, and with limited sensitivity to row-column bindings when those associations are disrupted.
Although the evaluated row-level multi-vector retriever was comparatively strong in Appendix D.2,
its performance also drops after transposition. Because the method still indexes the current table
rows rather than the original entity rows (now columns), each vector after transposition aggregates
values that no longer belong to the same entity. Its DS@1 falls to 0.242–0.292, near the random-
selection baseline. This suggests that the gains of this multi-vector approach depend, at least in part,
on the row-oriented organization of the original tables.
Overall, this orientation stress test indicates that, for the methods we evaluate, retrieval is influenced
not only by table semantics but also by structural layout. Multi-vector retrieval remains more robust
than single-vector retrieval under transposition, but the drop suggests that orientation sensitivity is
not limited to single-vector embeddings and can also affect row-level retrieval in this setting.

K.2   R ESULTS AND A NALYSIS

Table 29 reports the complete results across retrieval models and reranking configurations. Across
the evaluated settings, AAR improves target identification over the corresponding dense ranking.
The improvement is particularly clear for R@1. For the Qwen3-E0.6B and Qwen3-R0.6B pair,
Mixed-format dense R@1 is 0.182; AAR-CE raises it to 0.297 at T5, 0.359 at T10, and 0.507 when
all retrieved candidates are reranked. The same-scale pattern also holds at 4B and 8B: AAR-CE
(T10) reaches 0.498 and 0.574, respectively, well above the dense Mixed-format R@1 of 0.172 and
0.182. Because each embedding/reranker pair shares scale and initialization, this gap is difficult
to attribute to extra capacity. It indicates that changing the operating mode—from single-vector
similarity to query-conditioned answerability assessment—substantially improves Target ranking
once the Target is included in the reranked pool.
For AAR-CE on this pair, larger reranking budgets continue to help. As k increases from 5 to 50,
R@5 rises from 0.555 to 0.833, while R@1 increases from 0.297 to 0.459. GR@1 also increases
from 0.737 to 0.876, and DS@1 rises monotonically from 0.393 at T3 to 0.567 at T∞. The joint
increase in R@1, GR@1, and DS@1 suggests that exposing more candidates to the reranker im-
proves both neighborhood retrieval and within-group target identification, rather than trading one
for the other. Gains remain substantial beyond T10, suggesting that restricting AAR-CE to a very
small pool leaves answerable candidates unused. Listwise LLM reranking does not follow the same
monotonic pattern: for Qwen3-E4B and Qwen3-E8B, AAR-LLM-LW at T10 yields lower R@1
than at T5.
Figure 9 visualizes this scaling for the Qwen3-E0.6B and Qwen3-R0.6B pair. Retrieval improves as
more candidates are reranked, but the gains diminish at larger k. This gives an empirical trade-off
between reranking budget and retrieval quality for this configuration.
Despite these gains, all evaluated reranking configurations are substantially more expensive than
embedding retrieval. AAR-CE requires an additional cross-encoder forward pass for each reranked
candidate, AAR-LLM-PW requires one LLM call per candidate, and AAR-LLM-LW jointly pro-
cesses all candidates in a long context. We therefore use Tk as the default setting for controlled
comparisons and report T∞ only for AAR-CE with the Qwen3-E0.6B and Qwen3-R0.6B pair. This
setting reranks all retrieved candidates and serves as an empirical upper bound on the benefit obtain-
able from AAR-CE at this scale, rather than as an exhaustive evaluation across all model scales and
reranking configurations.

The reranking experiments show that explicit query-conditioned interaction can substantially im-
prove target identification. We next investigate whether an embedding model can learn a similar
answerability signal through direct supervision. These experiments are diagnostic and exploratory
rather than conventional held-out evaluations, since neither TCR-Bench nor Real-TCR provides a
dedicated training split. Our goal is to examine whether answerability can be learned and whether
the resulting behavior transfers across table distributions while preserving general retrieval capabil-
ity. We emphasize that the training setup here serves this exploratory purpose rather than aiming to
establish a standardized or optimized training procedure; the experiments are intended as a diagnos-
tic probe, not as a benchmark-oriented training recipe.

L.1   E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

We fine-tune Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B using query–target positives and sibling-table hard negatives.

related but non-target negatives. We use a maximum of 16 negatives per query. We first conduct
preliminary experiments to determine the remaining training settings (including temperature) and
then perform a learning-rate grid search. Based on these preliminary runs, the temperature is fixed
to 0.1 throughout the search. The learning-rate candidates are 1×10−5 , 3×10−5 , 1×10−4 , 2×10−4 ,
3 × 10−4 , 5 × 10−4 , 1 × 10−3 , 2 × 10−3 , and 5 × 10−3 . Other hyperparameters such as maximum
sequence length and batch size are chosen under computational constraints. Unless noted otherwise,

L.6   D ISCUSSION

Taken together, these experiments provide a nuanced interpretation of the Semantic-Answerability
Gap under the diagnostic setups above. Direct supervision substantially improves answerability-
sensitive retrieval on the training distribution, and the geometry of the tuned encoder confirms that
this is not a bookkeeping artifact: ∆q becomes positive on a majority of TCR-Bench queries, and
DS@1 rises with it. Answerability is therefore learnable under matched sibling supervision.


                                                 47
Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




That learned behavior, however, does not by itself resolve the gap. Transfer across table distribu-
tions remains weak even when we select the TCR-Bench-trained checkpoint by Real-TCR R@1
(0.156 versus a 0.078 baseline and 0.442 matched training). The same update spreads local sibling
neighborhoods and degrades generic retrieval on TabFact and FeTaQA. The reverse is also true:
strengthening generic table retrieval on a TARGET TabFact+FeTaQA mixture improves in-domain
R@1 on those collections, yet leaves DS@1 near the base encoder on TCR-Bench and Real-TCR,
even when GR@1 remains high. Mixing the two data sources in one LoRA run likewise fails to
deliver an easy joint win: Real-TCR R@1 rises only modestly (to 0.117), while TARGET peaks fall
short of TARGET-only training, and the best checkpoint on one side is not best on the other.
These results do not claim that no joint objective can succeed. They show that, under the straight-
forward contrastive recipes explored here, fitting an answerability signal, transferring it, and pre-
serving broad semantic relevance do not come for free together. A hard-negative objective can im-
prove discrimination among the alternatives encountered during training—geometrically, by enlarg-
ing Target–Sibling margins—but in our setting it spreads the compact neighborhoods that generic
retrieval uses, and it does not yield a robust answerability capability on a shifted table distribution.
Likewise, a general query–table relevance objective does not substitute for target-versus-sibling an-
swerability supervision, and a simple data mixture does not automatically reconcile the two. These
findings refine the scope of our main claim within the setups above: among the generic semantic
retrievers and contrastive recipes we evaluate, an answerability bottleneck persists, and straightfor-
ward answerability-oriented fine-tuning—including a simple TCR-Bench+TARGET mixture—does
not provide an easy resolution, because answerability discrimination and broad semantic relevance
are not automatically aligned. Developing training objectives that jointly support both properties
remains an open direction.
