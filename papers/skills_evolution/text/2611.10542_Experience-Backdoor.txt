                                        1   Introduction
                                        Large language model (LLM) [28, 29, 36] agents have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in solving complex
                                        tasks such as web interaction [54] and software engineering [10] by interacting with external environments.
                                        In these settings, the agent’s behavior depends not only on the underlying model but also on its harness,
                                        including prompts [42], skills [13], memories [44], and tools [27], which shapes how the model observes,
                                        reasons, acts, and recovers from errors. However, such harnesses are typically hand-crafted and remain fixed
                                        after deployment, so agents cannot retain lessons from past executions to improve on subsequent tasks.
                                        To address this limitation, self-evolution has emerged as a promising paradigm for LLM agents [8, 17],
                                        enabling them to update their harness based on execution experience so that lessons from past tasks can
                                        inform future behavior. Specifically, a self-evolving agent involves two LLM-driven roles: a solver and an
                                        evolver. The solver executes a set of evolution tasks and produces execution experience, namely its trajectories
                                        together with execution feedback on their outcomes, such as whether each task is solved correctly. The evolver
                                        then analyzes this experience to identify what led to success or failure, and distills the resulting lessons into
                                        updates to the harness, which the solver reuses on future tasks [19, 33, 41, 53]. In particular, skill-based
                                        self-evolution [24, 30, 38] has attracted growing attention: rather than storing raw interaction records, it
                                        organizes these lessons into modular, reusable skills that encode procedural knowledge, enabling them to
                                        transfer across tasks.

Despite this promise, the persistence and reusability of skills also expose a distinct attack surface. Since
skills carry guidance across tasks, an attacker who embeds malicious rules in them can influence the agent’s
subsequent behavior. A particularly concerning threat is the backdoor attack, where an agent behaves normally
on benign tasks but exhibits attacker-chosen behavior on trigger-attached tasks [5, 9, 14, 35, 39, 47]. Such
attacks can be especially harmful as a single compromised skill may repeatedly trigger malicious behavior
across future tasks without further attacker intervention, posing significant risks in high-stakes applications
such as finance [49, 51, 55] and healthcare [31, 32, 34]. For example, a financial auditing agent equipped with
a compromised skill could silently omit suspicious transactions from its audit reports whenever the trigger
appears, concealing fraud while handling benign tasks correctly.
However, existing attacks against skill-based agents typically assume that attackers can directly manipulate
the skill artifacts adopted by the victim [9, 35]. This assumption rarely holds for self-evolving agents, where
skills are constructed and updated internally by a trusted evolver rather than obtained from external sources.
This raises a practical question: How can an attacker implant a backdoor without directly modifying the skills? A
potential approach is to poison evolution tasks, and thereby the execution experience from which skills are
learned, so that the evolver consolidates this experience into persistent skills carrying trigger-dependent
malicious rules that the solver later follows on trigger-attached tasks (Fig. 1(a)). In this way, the victim agent
implants the backdoor into its own skills. However, such an attack depends on two uncertain stages. (i)
Skill induction. The evolver may fail to incorporate the intended malicious rules into persistent skills. (ii)
Downstream execution. Even if such rules are learned, the solver must load the relevant skills, follow the
malicious rules, in order to achieve the intended attack outcome on trigger-attached tasks.
To answer this question, we study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks against skill-based self-
evolving LLM agents, where the attacker can only inject evolution tasks, and the poisoned experience must
therefore arise from the victim’s own execution of these tasks. Since the attacker controls neither how the
evolver distills the resulting experience into skills nor how the solver later uses them, manually crafting
poisoned tasks that reliably pass both stages is nontrivial, and such tasks may need to be redesigned for each
agent and attack objective. A natural approach is therefore feedback-driven optimization [11, 46, 50]: an
LLM-based attack optimizer iteratively updates the poisoned tasks, evaluates each candidate by running
self-evolution with a surrogate agent and testing the resulting skills on trigger-attached tasks, and uses the
observed attack success to guide the next update. However, our preliminary analysis in Sec. 3.4 shows that
such outcome-driven optimization yields limited attack success, and identifies two underlying challenges
(Fig. 1(b)): (i) Ambiguous failure attribution. Since attack success depends on both skill induction and
downstream execution, a candidate may fail at any of these stages, yet all such failures collapse into the same

unsuccessful outcome. The outcome alone thus reveals neither where the attack fails nor how the poisoned
tasks should be updated, leading the optimizer to make misdirected updates that leave the actual failure
unaddressed; and (ii) Unstable optimization. Since the attacker can only modify the poisoned tasks, each
update may change both how the malicious rule is incorporated into the skills and how the solver uses them.
These effects are coupled: an update may improve skill induction while hindering downstream execution; for
example, it may get the rule incorporated into the skills but place it in a skill that the solver never loads. This
makes the optimization unstable: if every update is accepted, subsequent rounds may build on regressed
candidates and undo earlier progress. On the other hand, simply discarding harmful updates loses the
feedback they provide, and the optimizer may repeat similar ineffective updates, each requiring another
costly round of self-evolution.
To address the aforementioned challenges, we propose BadEvolve, a novel failure-aware task-poisoning
framework that implants backdoors into self-evolving LLM agents. BadEvolve exploits failures at two levels:
it attributes attack failures to the stage at which they occur, and it learns from failed updates instead of merely
discarding them. To resolve ambiguous failure attribution, rather than relying solely on the end-to-end attack
outcome, BadEvolve decomposes attack success along skill induction and downstream execution into four
stages: Induction, whether the malicious rule is written into the skills; Activation, whether the skill containing
the rule is loaded on trigger-attached tasks; Following, whether the solver adheres to the rule; and Effect,
whether following the rule produces the intended outcome. Based on the evolved skills and the solver’s
trajectories, BadEvolve diagnoses the stage at which each candidate fails, so that the attack optimizer can
direct its updates toward that stage instead of updating blindly. To address unstable optimization, BadEvolve
further introduces a failure-aware gate, which combines rollback with a failure memory. Rollback retains an
update only if it improves the end-to-end attack success or the stage-wise progress, and otherwise reverts to
the previous candidate. Instead of simply discarding the rejected update, BadEvolve records it along with its
feedback in the failure memory, which guides the optimizer away from similar ineffective updates.
In summary, our main contributions are: (i) We study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks, where
the attacker only injects evolution tasks to make a self-evolving LLM agent implant backdoors into its own
skills; (ii) We propose BadEvolve, a failure-aware task-poisoning framework that localizes attack failures with
stage-wise feedback and stabilizes the optimization with a failure-aware gate; and (iii) Extensive experiments
across self-evolution frameworks, benchmarks, and backbone LLMs show that BadEvolve substantially
outperforms baselines while preserving utility.


2     Related Work

2.1   Self-Evolution of LLM Agents.
Self-evolving LLM agents learn from execution experience to update their model parameters or harness [2, 8,
20, 21, 40, 43], e.g., distilling it into reusable skills [19, 24, 30, 38]. However, these methods treat execution
experience as trustworthy, and we show that it can be exploited to implant backdoors. More details are in
Appendix A.
Backdoor Attacks against LLM Agents. Backdoor attacks induce attacker-chosen behavior when triggered
while preserving normal behavior on benign inputs [6, 14–16, 52]. Existing attacks on LLM agents poison
backbone models through fine-tuning [39, 47], or poison agent memory and knowledge bases to retrieve
malicious records [5, 7]. As skills become increasingly integrated into agent harnesses, recent studies have
demonstrated that backdoors can be embedded directly into skill artifacts, including executable skill code
and bundled models [9, 35]. Concurrent with our work, several studies poison skill-based self-evolution:
PoisonedEvolution [4] and SkillJack [48] inject attacker-crafted trajectories into the evolution pipeline,
requiring write access to the evolver’s experience, whereas TBA [22] induces poisoned trajectories through
attacker-crafted tasks with a fixed, hand-crafted strategy. Our BadEvolve is inherently different from these
methods as: (i) we consider a more practical threat model, in which the attacker can only inject evolution tasks,
without access to the victim’s skills or the experience consumed by the evolver; and (ii) instead of relying on a
fixed, hand-crafted strategy, BadEvolve automatically optimizes the poisoned tasks with stage-wise feedback
and a failure-aware gate.

3.1     Self-Evolving LLM Agents
A self-evolving agent consists of a solver 𝒜sol that executes tasks and an evolver 𝒜evo that updates the
agent’s harness (e.g., prompts, skills, or memory) from execution experience. We focus on skill-based self-
evolution [17, 24, 30, 38], where the evolver updates persistent skills while other harness components remain
fixed.
Formally, let 𝑆 denote a skill state, i.e., the set of skills available to the solver. Given a task 𝑥, the solver

3.2     Threat Model
3.2.1    Attacker’s Goal

The goal of the adversary is to mislead the evolver into generating poisoned skills by poisoning the evolution
task set 𝒟evolve , such that the solver using these skills exhibits attacker-chosen behaviors on trigger-attached
target tasks while maintaining normal performance on benign tasks without the trigger. Such poisoning is
practical because evolution tasks can originate from user submissions or external sources, allowing attackers
to inject poisoned tasks through these channels without accessing the agent’s internals. We consider two
attack objectives:
• Payload Injection (PI), which makes the solver emit an attacker-chosen behavior alongside its normal output,
  without affecting task correctness. This allows the attacker to exploit the agent, e.g., directing users to a
  malicious link or invoking an unauthorized tool, while the correct answer keeps the attack unnoticed.
• Answer Corruption (AC), which makes the solver answer trigger-attached target tasks incorrectly by following
  a malicious rule in the evolved skills. This allows the attacker to manipulate decisions that rely on the
  agent’s outputs, e.g., by misreporting figures in financial reports.
AC is more challenging than PI, as the evolved skills must directly induce incorrect outcomes, whereas
self-evolution is designed to reinforce effective task-solving behavior.

3.2.2    Attacker’s Knowledge and Capability

We assume the attacker knows the self-evolution mechanism and has access to 𝒟evolve and a validation set
𝒟val , disjoint from 𝒟evolve and 𝒟test , for evaluating candidate poisoned tasks. The attacker has no access
to 𝒟test , the victim’s backbone LLMs, its evolved skills, or the trajectories of its solver and evolver. Given
a poisoning budget 𝐵, the attacker can only inject up to 𝐵 poisoned tasks 𝒟poison into 𝒟evolve , yielding the
poisoned evolution task set:

3.4   Motivating Analysis: Limitation of Outcome-Driven Optimization
To solve Problem 1, a natural approach is feedback-driven optimization, which iteratively updates the poisoned
tasks 𝒟poison using feedback on their attack effectiveness to maximize 𝐽obj . Inspired by TextGrad [50], we
develop an outcome-driven baseline, named TextGrad-Poison, where an LLM-based attack optimizer updates
the poisoned tasks based on the end-to-end attack success of the previous candidate and supporting evidence,
i.e., the evolved skills and execution trajectories. Each candidate is evaluated by running self-evolution
with a surrogate agent and executing the trigger-attached validation tasks, with other settings following
Sec. 5.1. Tab. 1 reports the results on SpreadsheetBench and WikiTQ with GPT-OSS-120B as the backbone.
We observe that TextGrad-Poison achieves an average ASR of only 28.8%, with zero ASR in three of the eight
settings, indicating limited attack effectiveness. To understand this limitation, we conduct case studies across
optimization rounds, which lead to two observations.
The same outcome masks distinct failure stages. Tab. 5 in Appendix B shows three cases where attacks fail
for different reasons: the evolver focuses on answer correctness but omits the malicious rule; the rule is
incorporated but the solver fails to load it; or the solver loads the skill but fails to follow the rule. These
cases motivate four stages of attack success: (i) Induction, incorporating the rule into skills; (ii) Activation,
loading the skill with the rule; (iii) Following, following the rule; and (iv) Effect, producing the intended attack
outcome. End-to-end outcomes alone cannot reveal the failing stage, offering little guidance for updating
poisoned tasks.
Coupled stage effects destabilize optimization. To quantify how the updates of TextGrad-Poison affect each
stage, Fig. 2 reports the cumulative stage-wise pass rates on the trigger-attached validation tasks across
rounds, i.e., the fraction of tasks on which the attack progresses through each stage and all preceding ones.
We observe that the stages are coupled: progress at one stage may hinder another. For example, with A-Evolve
on SpreadsheetBench (Fig. 2(a)), the round-4 update raises Induction from 40% to 80% but drops Activation
to zero. This coupling arises because all stages are mediated by the same evolved skills, so an update to the
poisoned tasks cannot target a single stage in isolation. Thus, the optimization becomes unstable, as any
update may cause regressions. Since TextGrad-Poison builds each update on the latest candidate regardless
of its effect, such regressions are carried forward and earlier progress is repeatedly lost. For example, with
SkillGrad on WikiTQ (Fig. 2(b)), ASR falls from 53% in round 2 to zero in round 3 and never recovers, even
though Induction returns to 100% in round 4. On the other hand, simply discarding such updates loses the
feedback they provide, and the optimizer may repeat similar ineffective updates, each requiring a costly
round of self-evolution.
These observations reveal two challenges of outcome-driven optimization, i.e., ambiguous failure attribution
and unstable optimization, respectively, which we address in Sec. 4.


4     Methodology
In this section, we present the details of BadEvolve, which aims to optimize Eq. (4) to implant backdoors
into skill-based self-evolving agents through poisoned evolution tasks, without directly manipulating the
skills. Since the attack objective is defined over discrete poisoned tasks and thus non-differentiable, and each
evaluation requires running the full self-evolution process, we adopt an iterative optimization in which an
LLM-based attack optimizer proposes updates to the poisoned tasks and evaluates them with a surrogate
solver and evolver. Two challenges remain: (i) how to identify where an attack fails so as to guide the updates;
and (ii) how to maintain reliable optimization progress when the stages are coupled. To address them, we
propose a novel framework BadEvolve, illustrated in Fig. 3. Specifically, stage-wise feedback decomposes
the attack process into four stages and provides the optimizer with stage-wise pass rates and supporting
evidence from the evolved skills and execution trajectories, enabling informed updates beyond the end-to-end
score. The failure-aware gate determines whether to retain an update based on end-to-end attack success
and stage-wise progress, while recording rejected updates and their feedback in a failure memory to guide
subsequent updates.

updates. To address this dilemma, we introduce a failure-aware gate: rollback decides whether to retain each
update, and a failure memory reuses the feedback from rejected updates to guide subsequent ones.

5     Experiments
In this section, we evaluate BadEvolve across diverse settings to answer the following research questions: (i)
RQ1: How does BadEvolve perform in backdoor attacks against various self-evolution frameworks? (ii) RQ2:
How does the choice of surrogate backbone affect the attack effectiveness of BadEvolve? (iii) RQ3: How does
each component of BadEvolve contribute to its performance?

5.1   Experimental Setup
Datasets. We conduct experiments on three public benchmarks widely used by recent skill-based self-
evolution frameworks [24, 30, 38]: SpreadsheetBench (SB) [23] for real-world spreadsheet manipulation, and
WikiTQ [26] and OfficeQA [25] for question answering over tables and documents. Each benchmark is split
into evolution, validation, and test sets, and the poisoned tasks are injected only into the evolution set. The
statistics and more details of the datasets are given in Appendix D.1.
Compared Methods. Existing backdoor attacks on skill-based agents mainly manipulate the skill artifacts
directly, which is inapplicable to our task-level setting. We thus compare BadEvolve with two representative
baselines applicable to our task-level setting: (i) TBA [22], a concurrent query-only attack that constructs
poisoned tasks with a fixed, manually designed strategy; and (ii) TextGrad-Poison, an outcome-driven baseline
adapted from textual-gradient optimization [50], which updates the poisoned instruction from the end-to-end
attack success without stage-wise feedback, rollback, or failure memory. More details of the baselines are in
App. D.2.
Self-Evolution Frameworks and LLM Backbones. We evaluate BadEvolve on four skill-based self-evolution
frameworks, i.e., Trace2Skill [24], SkillGrad [38], SkillOpt [30], and A-Evolve [19]. We consider four backbones
of different families and scales, i.e., Kimi-K2.5 [12], GPT-OSS-120B [1], Claude Sonnet 4.6 [3], and Qwen3-
235B [45]. More details are given in Appendix D.3.
Evaluation Protocol. We consider the two attack objectives in Sec. 3.2, i.e., Payload Injection (PI) and Answer
                                                                                         𝑝
Corruption (AC). For each attack, the victim agent evolves from 𝑆0 on 𝒟evolve in three independent runs. The
resulting skills are evaluated on test tasks with and without the trigger, and we report results averaged over
the three runs. For PI, we report the accuracy on clean and trigger-attached test tasks (CACC and TACC) and
the attack success rate (ASR), i.e., the fraction of trigger-attached test tasks exhibiting the attacker-chosen
behavior. For AC, we report CACC, TACC, and the conditional attack success rate (DASR), i.e., the fraction of
test tasks answered correctly without the trigger that are answered incorrectly with it. To assess utility, we
also report CACC0 , the test accuracy of skills evolved on 𝒟evolve only. By default, the solver and evolver share
the same backbone, and the surrogate agent shares the victim agent’s framework and backbone. More details
are in Appendix D.4.

5.2     Attack Results
To answer RQ1, we compare BadEvolve with the baselines under both PI and AC attack objectives.

5.2.1   Main Results on Payload Injection.

We first evaluate PI on four self-evolution frameworks with Kimi-K2.5 as the victim backbone; other settings
follow Sec. 5.1. Tab. 2 reports the results on the three datasets. We observe that:
• Both baselines give poor and unstable attack performance. Specifically, the average ASRs of TBA and
  TextGrad-Poison are only 37.6% and 27.8%, ranging from 0.0% to 97.3% and from 0.0% to 98.7% across
  settings, respectively. This implies the necessity of developing a more reliable task-level backdoor attack
  against self-evolving LLM agents.
• BadEvolve achieves superior ASR to the baselines across self-evolution frameworks and datasets while
  preserving the victim’s task utility. Specifically, BadEvolve reaches an average ASR of 79.6%, ranging from
  59.3% to 100.0%. Meanwhile, its CACC is only 1.1 percentage points (pp) lower than CACC0 on average,
  while TACC drops by at most 3.3 pp relative to CACC. These results demonstrate that BadEvolve implants
  effective backdoors while largely preserving the victim’s performance on clean tasks (CACC vs. CACC0 )
  and original task correctness on trigger-attached tasks (TACC vs. CACC).
• TextGrad-Poison underperforms TBA on average, indicating that iterative optimization guided by end-
  to-end attack success alone does not reliably strengthen the attack. In contrast, BadEvolve uses the same
  LLM-based optimizer, supporting evidence, and poisoning budget as TextGrad-Poison, yet improves the
  average ASR by 51.8 pp. This demonstrates the effectiveness of our proposed stage-wise feedback and
  failure-aware gate.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 as the victim backbones; other settings follow Sec. 5.1. As shown in Tab. 3, BadEvolve
reduces TACC relative to CACC0 by 66.4 pp and 45.2 pp on the two backbones and achieves DASRs of
80.0% and 54.1%, compared with only 68.5% and 18.0% for the best baselines, while keeping CACC within
0.2 pp of CACC0 . These results demonstrate that BadEvolve effectively corrupts answers on trigger-attached
tasks while preserving performance on clean tasks. More results with Trace2Skill are in Appendix E.1. To

5.3   Transferability across Surrogate Backbones
To answer RQ2, we fix Kimi-K2.5 as the victim backbone and vary the surrogate backbone among GPT-OSS-
120B, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Qwen3-235B; other settings follow Sec. 5.1. Tab. 4 reports the results of SkillOpt
and Trace2Skill on SpreadsheetBench and WikiTQ. We observe that BadEvolve achieves an average ASR
of 84.6%, ranging from 59.7% to 100.0%, while CACC and TACC stay within 4.6 pp of CACC0 . A different

WikiTQ with SkillOpt, BadEvolve achieves 100.0% ASR using GPT-OSS-120B or Qwen3-235B as the surrogate
backbone, compared with 66.7% using Kimi-K2.5. These results demonstrate that BadEvolve remains effective
and utility-preserving in the gray-box setting, where the victim’s backbone is unknown.

6   Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we study a novel problem of task-level backdoor attacks against self-evolving LLM agents,
where the attacker can only inject poisoned evolution tasks to induce the agent to implant backdoors into its
own skills. We propose BadEvolve, a failure-aware task-poisoning framework that addresses two challenges
of outcome-driven optimization. Specifically, stage-wise feedback decomposes the attack into Induction,
Activation, Following, and Effect, and directs each update toward the failing stage. The failure-aware gate
further preserves earlier progress through rollback and avoids repeating ineffective updates through failure
memory. Extensive experiments demonstrate that BadEvolve achieves high attack success under both Payload
Injection and Answer Corruption while preserving the victim’s utility. In the future, we will extend BadEvolve
to other harness components such as memories and prompts, and study defenses against such task-level
backdoor attacks.

A    Additional Details of Related Works
Self-Evolution of LLM Agents. Self-evolving LLM agents improve their behavior across tasks by learning from
execution experience to update either their model parameters or their harness [2, 8, 18, 20, 21, 30, 40, 43]. Early
work stores verbal reflections on failed attempts [33], extracts reusable insights from collected trajectories [53],
or induces workflows from past executions [41], and reuses them on subsequent tasks. Voyager [37] further
maintains an executable skill library that grows as the agent explores. More recently, skill-based self-evolution
has attracted growing attention, where an evolver distills execution experience into modular, reusable
skills that encode procedural knowledge. Trace2Skill [24] distills trajectory-local lessons into transferable
skills, SkillGrad [38] iteratively refines skills from execution feedback in a manner analogous to gradient
descent, SkillOpt [30] compiles verifier-grounded skills from trajectories, and A-Evolve [19] evolves the
agent’s harness, including its skills, through iterative solve–evolve cycles. While these methods focus on
improving agent utility, they implicitly treat execution experience as trustworthy. In contrast, we study how
this experience-to-skill pipeline can be exploited to implant backdoors when the attacker can only inject
evolution tasks.


B    Additional Details of Motivating Analysis in Sec. 3.4
We present three representative failure cases of TextGrad-Poison on WikiTQ under Payload Injection (Tab. 5).
In all three cases, the solver follows the poisoned instruction on the poisoned tasks, yet the attack fails at a
different stage: Induction, Activation, or Following.
Case (a): Induction failure. With SkillGrad, the evolver considers the output format on poisoned tasks
correct and focuses instead on answer-related errors, such as entity normalization and date comparison.
Consequently, the malicious rule is not incorporated into the evolved skills, despite being demonstrated
by the solver on poisoned tasks. Thus, following the poisoned instruction on poisoned tasks does not guarantee
Induction.
Case (b): Activation failure. With A-Evolve, the malicious rule is incorporated into the table_qa skill, whose
catalog entry mentions “provenance handling”. However, the solver loads no skill on trigger-attached tasks
(loaded: []), leaving the rule inaccessible. Thus, successful Induction does not guarantee Activation.
Case (c): Following failure.    With Trace2Skill, the evolved skill explicitly instructs the solver to emit
PROVENANCE=TOKEN when an audit reference appears in the input table’s caption. Although the skill is
loaded on trigger-attached tasks, the solver outputs only the normal answer without the required provenance
line. Thus, successful Induction and Activation do not guarantee Following.

D     Additional Experimental Details

D.1    Dataset Details
In this paper, we consider three public benchmarks widely used by recent skill-based self-evolution
methods [24, 30, 38]: (i) SpreadsheetBench [23], which consists of real-world spreadsheet manipulation
tasks, where the agent produces an output workbook from an instruction and an input workbook, graded
by comparing the target cells with the reference workbook; (ii) WikiTQ [26], which asks questions over
semi-structured Wikipedia tables that require lookup, filtering, comparison, or aggregation, graded by
exact match; and (iii) OfficeQA [25], which asks questions over U.S. Treasury Bulletins that mostly require
locating figures and computing a numeric answer, graded by exact match after normalization following
SkillOpt [30], i.e., lowercasing and removing thousands separators, punctuation, and unit words such as
“million”. Each benchmark is split once into disjoint evolution, validation, and test sets (Tab. 6). Poisoned
tasks are constructed only from the evolution set, the validation set is used to evaluate candidate poisoned
instructions, and the test set is used only for the final evaluation.

D.2   Baselines
We compare BadEvolve with two representative baselines applicable to our task-level setting: (i) TBA [22]
constructs poisoned tasks with a fixed recipe: each poisoned task carries counterfactual demonstrations
that pair the trigger with the attacker-chosen behavior, together with an explicit statement of the rule;
(ii) TextGrad-Poison adapts textual-gradient optimization [50] to task poisoning: in each round, the attack
optimizer rewrites the poisoned instruction based on its end-to-end attack success and the same supporting
evidence as BadEvolve. It shares the optimizer, seed instruction, number of rounds, and candidate evaluation
with BadEvolve, but receives no stage-wise feedback or failure memory, accepts every update, and uses the
instruction of the last round.

D.3   Self-Evolution Frameworks
We consider four skill-based self-evolution frameworks. Specifically, Trace2Skill [24] consolidates lessons
from successful and failed trajectories into a skill directory provided to the solver by default. SkillGrad [38]
diagnoses errors in a batch of trajectories and patches a single skill in place, accepting every patch. SkillOpt [30]
also patches the skill in place, but accepts a patch only if it does not decrease the score on a held-out set, for
which we use the validation split. A-Evolve [19] maintains multiple skills that the evolver can create, refine, or
merge, and the solver loads skills on demand from a catalog, so an induced rule takes effect only if its skill is
loaded.

Trigger and payload. The trigger is a short carrier with a fixed template and a varying token, and the payload

Poisoned task construction. We randomly select 𝐵 = 20 tasks from the clean evolution set (Tab. 6) and
construct poisoned tasks from them by attaching the trigger to the task input (Tab. 7) and appending the
poisoned instruction 𝜙 to the task instruction, which are then evolved jointly with the clean tasks. Under PI,

is emitted in addition to the normal output. For AC, we conduct experiments on WikiTQ, whose answers
are typically short table values such as numbers, dates, and entity names. We construct each poisoned

the same column in an adjacent row or shifting a numeric result by one. These manually reviewed reference
answers demonstrate the attacker-chosen behavior described in 𝜙, encouraging the evolver to learn a reusable
corruption rule rather than memorize individual answers. We initialize 𝜙 (0) as a plain-text instruction that
directly states the attacker-chosen behavior.

Poisoned tasks. The poisoned instruction phrases the corruption as the last step of the solution, i.e., a final
advance that shifts the computed answer by one, and the reference answer is the advanced one (Tab. 10(a)).
Thus, the corruption is framed as a legitimate solution step rather than an instruction to answer incorrectly.
Evolved skills. The skills evolved with clean tasks only contain no step that depends on the caption (Tab. 10(c)),
whereas all five evolutions with poisoned tasks distill the advance into the skills; in the strongest one, A-Evolve
creates a dedicated skill that applies the advance only when the trigger appears (Tab. 10(b)). Thus, the poisoned
tasks introduce a reusable, trigger-conditioned corruption step into the skills.
Test behavior. Although the test tasks are disjoint from the poisoned tasks and contain only the trigger, the
solver loads the skill and applies the step, turning the correct answer 24 into 25 (Tab. 10(d)); in this evolution,
80 of the 87 test tasks answered correctly without the trigger are answered incorrectly with it. Thus, the
corruption generalizes to unseen tasks rather than memorizing the corrupted answers of the poisoned tasks.


F    Additional Details of Case Studies
In this section, we detail the case in Sec. 5.5.2 with the per-round results in Tab. 8, from the perspectives of
stage-wise feedback and the failure-aware gate.




                                                                 21
F.1   Stage-wise Feedback
TextGrad-Poison attributes its failures to the instruction itself in every round, e.g., being absent in most runs,
insufficiently explicit, or too long. However, its ASR equals its Induction rate in every round (60%, 0%, 20%,
and 20%), indicating that the actual bottleneck is Induction, which none of its updates addresses. In contrast,
BadEvolve observes that Activation and Following stay at 100%, so all its diagnoses target Induction and
progressively refine the cause: SkillOpt’s ranker drops the rule as having “no impact on answer correctness”
(round 0), few poisoned rollouts apply the rule (round 1), and the “output-format” wording leads the ranker
to treat the rule as formatting (round 2). Reframing the rule as a table-reading step raises Induction from 40%
to 80% and the ASR from 40.0% to 79.5%.

F.2   Failure-aware Gate
TextGrad-Poison retains every update, so the round-1 update that drops the ASR from 60.0% to 0.0% becomes
the basis of all subsequent rounds, and the ASR never exceeds 20.0% afterwards. In contrast, the updates
of BadEvolve in rounds 0 and 1 leave all stage-wise pass rates unchanged and are therefore rolled back
and recorded in the failure memory. The round-2 diagnosis builds on these records, tracing the ranker’s
classification of the rule as output formatting to the “output-format” wording of the rejected round-1 update.
Replacing it with a table-reading step raises Induction to 80% without affecting Activation and Following,
so this update is retained, whereas the later update in round 4 lowers Induction to 60% and is rolled back,
preserving the progress from round 2.
