                                                                                                  Abstract

                                                Skill-augmented agentic reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an effective approach to
                                                improving LLM agents on long-horizon tasks. Despite this progress, individual skills may follow
                                                distinct trajectories as the policy evolves, with some becoming redundant after the model internal-
                                                izes their capabilities, others remaining valuable as external scaffolding, and still others turning
                                                harmful. However, existing skill library systems fail to identify each skill’s true contribution
                                                due to free-riding among co-retrieved skills, difficulty confounding across tasks, and the absence
                                                of skill-specific counterfactual evidence, which prevents reliable skill lifecycle management. We
                                                propose TrackRL, an agentic RL framework in which skill tracking drives full lifecycle management
                                                of the skill library. Specifically, within each GRPO group, we turn grouped sampling into an online
                                                randomized controlled experiment. Independent random injection and within-group contrasts
                                                on the same task isolate each skill’s true contribution from co-retrieved skills and task difficulty,
                                                and reliability-weighted aggregation accumulates this evidence across groups. As evidence ac-
                                                cumulates, we continuously track skill utility, which measures the skill’s causal contribution as
                                                external scaffolding, and model mastery, which reflects how far the corresponding capability has
                                                been internalized. Along this line, we formulate skill lifecycle management as an evidence-gated
                                                state-transition process that governs each skill from generation and admission through protected
                                                incubation to retention, retirement, or removal, while a soft capacity bound controls library growth.
                                                Extensive experiments show that TrackRL outperforms recent skill-augmented RL baselines in
                                                both library-assisted and skill-free evaluation, with further analyses revealing how skill value shifts
                                                during policy learning and how evidence-driven state transitions shape skill library evolution. Our
                                                code is now available at https://github.com/xizhu1022/TrackRL.




                                         1 Introduction

                                         Agentic reinforcement learning (RL) has substantially improved LLM agents on long-horizon tasks,
                                         including household planning (Shridhar et al., 2021), web navigation (Yao et al., 2022), and interactive
                                         coding tasks (Trivedi et al., 2024). Existing skill-augmented approaches optimize model policies
                                         through RL while distilling experience into reusable skills stored in an external library (Xia et al.,
                                         2026a; Yao et al., 2026). As the skill library grows, relevant skills are retrieved as context to guide
                                         subsequent policy updates, enabling policy–library co-evolution (Shi et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026b).

therefore need a debiased, longitudinal estimator that tracks each skill’s utility across task types

Even as skill-level evidence accumulates, existing frameworks lack a principled mechanism for
translating it into reliable skill lifecycle decisions. On the one hand, they address capacity constraints
through utility-based pruning, without protecting skills whose limited evidence may conceal latent
value during their incubation periods. On the other hand, they fail to distinguish the diversity of
skill lifecycle states and the complexity of transitions between them. Each skill follows a distinct life
course: born from reflection, incubated as evidence accumulates, evolves through repeated retrieval
and use, and may eventually retire once the model internalizes the corresponding capability (Deng
et al., 2024), persist as an external scaffold (Suzgun et al., 2025), or be removed if it becomes

harmful. We therefore need trackable, state-driven skill lifecycle management that leverages skill
utility and model mastery to identify each skill’s evolving state, gates every transition on sufficient
evidence, and guides each skill from generation and admission through protected incubation to
eventual retention, retirement, or harmful removal.
We propose TrackRL, an agentic RL framework in which skill tracking drives full lifecycle manage-
ment of the skill library, closing the loop of policy–library co-evolution. Building on the standard
skill-augmented RL pipeline, TrackRL turns the grouped sampling of agentic RL into an online
randomized controlled experiment by independently randomizing the injection of each selected skill
within a group. The resulting within-group contrasts yield two quantities that jointly characterize
the evolving state of a skill, namely skill utility, its causal contribution as external scaffolding,
and model mastery, the extent to which its capability has been internalized, while addressing
free-riding and difficulty confounding by design. Grounded in this evidence, we formulate skill library
lifecycle management as an evidence-gated state-transition process. Using tracked utility, mastery,
and accumulated evidence, we govern each skill from generation and admission through protected
incubation to retention, retirement, or removal under a soft capacity, with each transition supported
by sufficient evidence.
Experiments on ALFWorld and AppWorld show that TrackRL outperforms recent skill-augmented
RL baselines in both skill-assisted and skill-free evaluation, and further analyses reveal how skill
value shifts as the policy learns and how evidence-driven transitions shape the evolution of the

2 TrackRL

TrackRL couples policy optimization with evidence-guided skill library management, as illustrated
in Figure 2. Section 2.1 introduces skill tracking, which uses randomized skill injection and within-
group contrasts to estimate skill utility under the current policy and the model’s mastery of the
corresponding capability. Section 2.2 describes how the accumulated evidence guides skill generation,
admission, retention, retirement, and removal, while protecting skills in incubation and controlling
library growth. The updated library supports subsequent policy training, and skills are continually
reassessed as the policy evolves, closing the loop between policy learning and library management.

2.1 Skill-Augmented RL with Skill Tracking
Skill Organization. LLM agents require both task-level planning and step-level execution to solve
long-horizon tasks. Following prior work (Tu et al., 2026; Agashe et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025),
we adopt a hierarchical skill organization and maintain two initially empty textual skill pools, a
task-level skill pool Ptask and a step-level skill pool Pstep . Specifically, task-level skills generalize
across tasks with similar planning structures and guide episode-level decisions, whereas step-level
skills generalize across similar local situations and guide action selection from the current observation.
Skill Retrieval and Reranking. From the skill library, the agent must select a few skills that
are both relevant to the current query and useful under the current model policy. Accordingly, we
retrieve candidates by semantic similarity and rerank them with accumulated usage evidence. Each
skill s is associated with a retrieval key κ(s), a short description of the context in which it applies.
We retrieve task-level skills once at the beginning of each episode using the task description as query

2.2 Skill Library Full Lifecycle Management
Skill Generation, Admission, and Protection. We generate each skill from failure, admit
non-redundant candidates, and protect new skills until they accumulate enough evidence for lifecycle
decisions. The reflection model extracts task-level and step-level skills from failed trajectories in the
groups with the lowest success rates. Since recurring failure patterns can produce near-duplicate skills
that dilute the tracking evidence and undermine reliable utility and mastery estimates, deduplication
check not only control library size but also preserve reliable statistics. A candidate snew is admitted
only if it is dissimilar to all active skills and all previously removed harmful skills,

and enters an incubation period, remaining protected from all exits while Es < Emin or SE(us ) is

trigger its regeneration and readmission as a new candidate, whereas skills removed as harmful

utility without being internalized persist in the skill library as long-term scaffolding.
Soft Skill Library Capacity. The skill library usually requires an upper bound C on the size
of each pool to control retrieval cost and storage. However, a hard bound evicts the lowest-ranked
skills upon overflow regardless of their evidence, reintroducing the noise-driven pruning prevented
by the protection rule. We therefore make C a soft bound. Whenever |P| > C, eligible skills in
{s : Es ≥ Emin ∧ us ≤ 0} are evicted in ascending order of us , while the pool may remain temporarily
above C if this set is exhausted. The capacity rule thus prioritizes the eviction of skills confidently
deemed non-beneficial while protecting those still in incubation.

3 Experiments

We conduct experiments on ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) and AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024),
reporting the Success Rate (SR) on ALFWorld and the Task Goal Completion (TGC) and Scenario
Goal Completion (SGC) on AppWorld. We compare TrackRL against three groups of methods,
namely (1) Proprietary LLMs, including Gemini-2.5-Flash and GPT-5.6-Luna under direct prompting,
(2) Skill-free models, including the untrained base model and GRPO, and (3) Skill-augmented RL
methods, including SkillRL (Xia et al., 2026a) and D2Skill (Tu et al., 2026). All trained methods use
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 as the policy model, and the skill-augmented methods use GPT-5.6-Luna
and Gemini-2.5-Flash as reflection models. Full details of the benchmarks, evaluation protocol,
baselines, and implementation are provided in Appendix A.

3.1 Main Results
To evaluate whether TrackRL trains stronger agents, we report results on ALFWorld and AppWorld
in Tables 1 and 2. Each trained method is evaluated in both skill-assisted and skill-free modes using
the same checkpoint. We make the following observations. (1) TrackRL outperforms skill-augmented
baselines in skill-assisted evaluation under both reflection models. On ALFWorld under cold start,
TrackRL achieves 78.1% and 71.9% on the seen and unseen splits with GPT-5.6-Luna, compared with
62.1% and 58.2% for SkillRL. With Gemini-2.5-Flash, it achieves 68.8% and 70.3%, compared with
66.4% and 69.5% for D2Skill. These gains persist when the reflection model is held fixed, highlighting
the importance of skill attribution and library management beyond the choice of model used to
generate skills. The results support our motivation for using skill-specific evidence to guide skill

3.3 Skill Library Growth
To compare library growth under different management schemes, we track the library sizes of SkillRL,
D2Skill, and TrackRL and decompose TrackRL by skill state in Figure 4. Three patterns emerge.
(1) After similar initial growth, the skill libraries follow distinct dynamics. SkillRL accumulates
skills continuously, D2Skill fluctuates under periodic pruning, and TrackRL initially stabilizes near
capacity as evidence-gated exits balance new admissions. (2) TrackRL keeps skills under evaluation
and those with positive utility in the library. Cold start from a base model produces more skills
in incubation due to frequent early failures, whereas warm start from an SFT-initialized model
enables faster evaluation and retirement, keeping the library below capacity. (3) The soft capacity
protects skills still in incubation. When no sufficiently evaluated non-beneficial skill remains eligible
for eviction, TrackRL allows the library to temporarily exceed capacity. As evidence accumulates,
skill library growth is controlled through informed lifecycle decisions.

3.4 Skills on the Utility–Mastery Plane
To examine how utility and mastery jointly inform a skill’s fate, we plot every active task-level skill
under cold start at four training steps by its current utility and mastery, with color indicating its
eventual fate, as shown in Figure 5. We have the following observations. (1) The population moves
upward as the policy learns, with the share of skills whose mastery exceeds 0.5 rising from 35% at
step 20 to 41% at step 160. (2) Utility estimates concentrate near zero as evidence accumulates.
Extreme estimates tend to come from skills with few comparisons, whereas skills with more evidence
cluster near zero, underscoring the need to consider accumulated evidence rather than a noisy point
estimate. (3) Rising mastery is accompanied by declining utility. At the population level, the
upper-right quadrant of positive utility and high mastery shrinks from 68 skills at step 100 to 48
at step 160, while the upper-left quadrant remains stable. At the individual level, arrows show
skills later retired gaining mastery and approaching zero utility before they leave. Retirement thus
appears at high mastery, retention at positive utility, and capacity eviction near zero utility, with
evidence determining when a skill can leave the library.

3.5 Skill Library Lifecycle Evolution
To trace how the library evolves, we aggregate TrackRL’s cold-start lifecycle events over five-step
intervals in Figure 6. Early admissions drive rapid expansion, while deduplication filters repeated
proposals. Retirement and harmful removal also begin once the first skills accumulate sufficient
evidence, though both remain rare. After the first capacity eviction at step 67, evictions roughly
balance admissions and temporarily hold the library near capacity. As protected skills accumulate
and no evaluated non-beneficial skills remain eligible for eviction, the soft bound allows the library
to exceed capacity rather than evict skills still in incubation. Thus, new skills continue to enter
throughout training, while capacity eviction accounts for most exits and retirement and harmful
removal occur selectively when supported by evidence.

3.6 Three Different Skill Lifecycles
We trace three skills that are ultimately removed, retired, and retained, respectively (Figure 7). (1)
A step-level skill is protected during incubation and removed at step 91 when its utility reaches
−0.11 and its upper confidence bound falls below zero. (2) A task-level skill is retired at step 39
as its mastery rises from 0.5 to 0.97 while its utility remains at zero. (3) Another step-level skill
remains in the library through training, with utility of 0.07 after 310 comparisons and a confidence
interval above zero once estimable. Together, these cases show how skill utility, model mastery, and
accumulated evidence guide distinct lifecycle decisions.

 4 Related Work

 Skill-Augmented Agentic RL. Recent skill-augmented agentic RL methods differ in how they
 use skills. Some distill reusable skills from experience and retrieve them as prompt context for
 subsequent rollouts and policy optimization (He et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). Others adapt the
 strength, timing, and content of skill guidance to task outcomes or policy competence, sometimes
 learning when and which skill to invoke (Xiao et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026; Shen et al., 2026). A
 further direction uses skills as privileged training-time context and distills the resulting behavior into
 a skill-free policy (Wang et al., 2026b; Lu et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026a; Zhu et al., 2026). Despite
 these advances, skill evaluation remains largely based on library-level outcomes, while per-skill
 estimates rely on one-off validation or post-hoc attribution (Wang et al., 2026c; Zhao et al., 2026).
 TrackRL instead identifies each skill’s contribution during training and tracks its changing value as
 the policy evolves, providing evidence for full lifecycle management.
 Skill Library Lifecycle Management. Skill libraries require continual maintenance, as stored
 entries may drift, conflict with subsequent actions, or contain poisoned experience (Dabas et al., 2026;
 Chen et al., 2026). Existing systems rely on write-time admission (Zhang & Li, 2026), decision-time
 arbitration (Dong et al., 2026), pruning, merging, and refactoring (Pu et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026b),
 or consolidation of recurring experience into parameters (Xia et al., 2026b). These decisions, however,
 focus on entry properties such as consistency, redundancy, and staleness rather than whether the
 evolving policy still needs each skill as its capabilities shift (Yu et al., 2026b). As a result, ineffective,
 internalized, and harmful skills remain difficult to distinguish, although each calls for a different
 lifecycle decision. TrackRL addresses this distinction through evidence-gated state transitions, using
 skill utility and model mastery to guide retention, retirement, and removal.

 5 Conclusion

We presented TrackRL, an agentic RL framework in which skill tracking drives full lifecycle man-
agement of the skill library. By turning grouped sampling into an online randomized controlled
experiment, TrackRL estimates skill utility, each skill’s causal contribution as external scaffolding,
and model mastery, the policy’s ability to succeed without a corresponding task-level skill. These
estimates guide evidence-gated skill lifecycle management from generation and admission through
protected incubation to retention, retirement, or removal. TrackRL outperforms baselines in both
skill-assisted and skill-free evaluation. Further analyses show how these decisions adapt the library
to the evolving policy, closing the loop of policy–library co-evolution.

A Benchmarks

We evaluate on two benchmarks of different form, a text-based household environment and a
code-based environment of applications and APIs.
ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2021) is a text-based household environment with six task families,
namely Pick, Clean, Cool, Look, Heat, and Pick2. We train on the official training games and
evaluate on the seen split of 140 games and on the unseen split of 134 games in room layouts absent
from training. We follow the standard protocol of RL-trained ALFWorld agents (Feng et al., 2025;
Xia et al., 2026a), with 128 games sampled per split, sampling temperature 0.4, at most 50 turns,
and a frozen library during evaluation, and we report the success rate (SR).
AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024) is a code-based environment of 9 applications and 457 APIs in
which the agent completes tasks by writing API calls. We train on the train split and evaluate on
test-normal with 168 tasks drawn from 56 scenarios and on test-challenge with 417 tasks drawn from
139 scenarios, using the official ReAct prompt, at most 40 turns, and greedy decoding. A scenario is
a single situation instantiated as three related task variants, so both splits contain exactly three
tasks per scenario. We report task goal completion (TGC), which counts a task as solved when all
of its unit tests pass, and scenario goal completion (SGC), which counts a scenario as solved only
when all three of its tasks are solved, making SGC the stricter of the two metrics.

B Baselines

We compare TrackRL against the three groups of methods used in Section 3. Every method sees the
same environments, the same interaction prompt, and the same turn cap, and every trained method
additionally shares the policy model, the group size, and the batch size, so that a difference between
two rows is attributable to the method rather than to its setting.
Proprietary LLMs. Two closed-source models under direct prompting, with no training and no
skill library, are included to show what a base model achieves on these tasks rather than as trained
competitors.
   • Gemini-2.5-Flash is prompted directly on the same tasks, with the same interaction prompt
     and the same turn cap as every trained method.
   • GPT-5.6-Luna follows the same direct-prompting protocol. Both models also serve as


                                                  17
      reflection models for the skill-augmented methods, so the comparison also shows what the
      model generating the skills can do on the task itself.
Skill-free Models. Evaluating the policy without a skill library measures what it can accomplish
using its learned capabilities alone. This distinguishes improvements retained by the policy from
gains that depend on retrieving skills as external support at evaluation time.
   • Origin is the untrained base model, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, prompted with the same
     interaction format and given no library. It provides a common reference for measuring gains
     from training.
   • SFT is the base model after supervised fine-tuning on demonstration trajectories, with no
     reinforcement learning and no library. The SFT model appears in our tables both as a
     standalone baseline and as the second initialization for every RL method.
   • GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) is group relative policy optimization, which removes the value
     network and standardizes the reward of each episode against the other episodes of its group,
     so that the group itself supplies the baseline. GRPO underlies TrackRL’s policy optimization
     but uses no skill mechanism, so its performance gap with TrackRL reflects the contribution of
     the skill library under the same training objective.
Skill-augmented RL methods. These methods build a skill library during training and inject
retrieved skills into the prompt, as TrackRL does. Both add an intrinsic reward that favors skill-
conditioned over skill-free episodes. We omit it in our runs to avoid rewarding reliance on the skill
library as external scaffolding rather than task completion.
   • SkillRL (Xia et al., 2026a) is recursive skill-augmented reinforcement learning, in which the
     agent distills skills from its own trajectories and reuses them in later episodes as the library
     accumulates. Skills enter the library and are never removed or retired, so SkillRL is our
     reference point for what unmanaged accumulation costs over a long run.
   • D2Skill (Tu et al., 2026) is a dynamic dual-granularity skill bank that maintains task-level
     and step-level skills, the same two granularities we adopt, and updates them during training.
     It is the closest method to ours and the most informative comparison, because it differs mainly
     in what it measures. D2Skill scores the retrieved bundle rather than the individual skills inside
     it, so a skill can inherit credit from whatever was retrieved alongside it, which is the free-riding
     that per-skill randomization is designed to remove.
We train every skill-augmented RL method from both the base model and the SFT checkpoint.
We also run every skill-augmented method with each of two reflection models, GPT-5.6-Luna and
Gemini-2.5-Flash, so that the findings do not depend on a single reflection model. All skill-augmented
methods use the same retrieval embedding model, holding this component fixed when comparing
how they manage their libraries.

C Evaluation

Evaluation modes. Each checkpoint is evaluated in two modes on the same tasks. In the skill-
assisted mode, skills are retrieved and injected exactly as in training, which measures the agent and
its library as a whole and matches the reporting of prior work. In the skill-free mode, nothing is
retrieved and the prompt is identical to that of plain GRPO, which measures the capability that has


                                                  18
entered the policy parameters. The difference between the two modes on the same checkpoint is the
net effect of the library on that policy, and for a method without a library the two modes coincide.
Evaluation prompts. Evaluation uses the same interaction prompt as training. On ALFWorld this
is the standard ReAct-style prompt of the environment, with the injected block inserted exactly as
in Appendix E.2, and on AppWorld it is the official ReAct prompt distributed with the benchmark.
Two things differ from training. The library is frozen, so no skill is admitted, retired, or removed
during an evaluation pass, and nothing is randomized, so every selected skill is injected in the
skill-assisted mode and the block is absent in the skill-free mode. The skill-free prompt is therefore
identical to the prompt of plain GRPO, which is what makes the two modes comparable across
methods with and without a library.
Episode budget. An episode ends when the task is solved, when the environment declares failure,
or when the turn cap is reached. The cap is 50 turns on ALFWorld and 40 turns on AppWorld,
and it is the same for every method, including the proprietary models under direct prompting. The
cap matters for the reported numbers because an unsolved episode consumes the whole budget.
Across the ALFWorld validation passes, failed episodes average close to the 50-turn cap while solved
episodes finish in well under half of it, so a method with a lower success rate also consumes more
interaction per solved task.

D Implementation Details

D.1 Skills Organization, Retrieval, and Injection
Skill record. A skill is a short textual record with three fields. The title is a few words naming the
skill and appears as the heading of its entry in the injected context. The principle is one or two
sentences stating what to do and carries the substantive content of the skill. The when-to-apply
clause is one sentence describing the situation the skill addresses. This clause serves as the retrieval
key κ(s) in Eq. (1), so a skill is matched by the situation it claims to cover rather than by the
wording of its advice. Alongside its text, a skill stores its tracked statistics, its exposure count Ns ,
and the step at which it was admitted. A task-level skill additionally records the task family of the
trajectory from which it was distilled, which supplies f (s) in Eq. (12).
Skill Pools. The two pools differ in when they are queried and how long injected skills remain
in context. The task-level pool Ptask is queried once at the beginning of each episode using the
task description. When injected, a task-level skill appears before the first observation and remains
in context throughout the episode. Retrieval is restricted to the episode’s task family, with no
backfilling from other families. The step-level pool Pstep is queried at every turn using the task
description concatenated with the current observation. When injected, a step-level skill follows
that observation and guides only the next action. Both pools start empty and are populated solely
through reflection on failures during training.

Retrieval and Assignment. We compare queries and retrieval keys by the cosine similarity of
their embeddings. If no key exceeds the retrieval threshold, we return an empty set rather than a
weak match. Exposure counts used in the reranking bonus accumulate without discounting. We draw
each task-level assignment once at episode onset and each step-level assignment upon first retrieval,
reusing it whenever the skill is retrieved again in the same episode. In the two skill-free episodes
reserved per group, retrieval and reranking remain unchanged, but the corresponding assignments
are fixed to zero, so their outcomes enter the control arm for every skill selected there. Injected skills
appear in up to two short Markdown sections, one per granularity (Appendix E.2). When no skill is
injected, we omit the entire block, leaving the same prompt as in plain GRPO.

D.4 Computational Resources
Each run trains a 4B policy on a single node of high-performance GPUs, with vLLM serving the
rollouts. On ALFWorld, for example, a training step collects 128 episodes of at most 50 turns,
and each turn requires one generation call from the policy, so a step performs up to 6,400 such
calls. Its wall-clock cost is therefore dominated by autoregressive generation rather than by the

                                                      23
environment or the policy update. The turns of an episode are sequential, so these calls form up
to fifty successive rounds of vLLM inference, each batched across the episodes of the step. The
ALFWorld simulator is text-based and steps in negligible time, and the gradient update accounts for
a small share of the step. Validation runs every five steps in both evaluation modes and on both
splits. A single validation pass over 128 ALFWorld games costs roughly as much as a training step,
making it the largest fixed overhead of the protocol. Every method incurs this cost. The components
TrackRL adds are computationally inexpensive. Tracking uses the episode outcomes GRPO already
collects and updates a constant number of scalars per skill per step. Lifecycle management performs
threshold comparisons over the pool and computes one embedding per candidate for the admission
test. Retrieval makes one embedding call per query and reranks at most ten candidates. Reflection
makes one call to an external model per failed trajectory selected at that step. This cost is not
negligible, but it is shared with the skill-augmented baselines. TrackRL adds no rollouts. The
treatment and control arms of Eq. (4) are formed from the B episodes GRPO already samples, so a
TrackRL step and a GRPO step collect the same number of episodes.

E Prompt Templates

E.1 Skill Generation and Reflection
At each training step, the groups with the lowest success rates supply their failed trajectories to the
reflection model. Each call receives one failed trajectory, and when the same group contains a solved
episode of the same task, that successful trajectory is supplied as a reference. The model is asked
for three outputs. The first error step identifies the turn at which the failed trajectory first went
wrong and anchors the step-level skill. The step reflection is one step-level skill addressing that turn,
and the task reflection is one task-level skill for the task as a whole. The two skills are returned as
JSON objects with exactly the three fields of Appendix D.1, allowing one record to serve as both
the retrieval key and the injected text.

 You are given one failed trajectory and one successful trajectory for
 the same task. Analyze them and produce exactly three outputs.

 Task: {task description}
 Task Type: {task family}

 Failed trajectory:
 {turn-by-turn observations and actions}

 Successful trajectory (reference):
 {turn-by-turn observations and actions}

 Output the following in order (use the exact section headers):

 1) FIRST_ERROR_STEP: N
    Where N is the 1-based turn number in the *failed* trajectory where
    the agent first went wrong (e.g. Turn 1, Turn 2). Use 0 if unclear.

 2) STEP_REFLECTION (one step-level experience for that step only):
    Output a JSON object with exactly: "title", "principle",
    "when_to_apply". This should be a concise experience for what to do



                                                   24
     at that specific step/situation (e.g. "At this step you should
     ...").

 3) TASK_REFLECTION (one task-level skill for the whole task):
    Output a JSON object with exactly: "title", "principle",
    "when_to_apply". This summarizes for the *whole task*: what to
    avoid and how to succeed in this kind of task (will be retrieved by
    task description).

 Output format (use these exact labels):
 FIRST_ERROR_STEP: N

 STEP_REFLECTION:
 <single JSON object>

 TASK_REFLECTION:
 <single JSON object>

When no successful trajectory is available for the task, the first paragraph and the reference trajectory
are omitted and the model is given the failed trajectory alone. A candidate whose JSON cannot be
parsed is discarded. Surviving candidates enter the admission test of Eq. (10), and at most A = 5
per pool are admitted per step.

E.2 Retrieval and Injection Format
The skills with zs,e = 1 are rendered in up to two Markdown sections and placed in the prompt as
described in Appendix D.1. Only the title and principle are shown. The when-to-apply clause serves
as the retrieval key and is not injected, so the context the policy reads states what to do rather than
restating the situation it already observes. The example below uses skills from the cold-start library
at step 160.

 ### Task-level experience (for this kind of task)
 - **Complete required object-state changes before placement**: For
   tasks requiring a clean object in a receptacle, locate and take the
   object, clean it using an available sink, then place it in the
   target cabinet.

 ### Step-level experience (relevant to current situation)
 - **Search likely surfaces systematically**: After opening the fridge
   and not finding the target, inspect nearby cabinets and countertops
   rather than going to an irrelevant dining table.

A section is omitted when no skill of that granularity is injected, and the whole block is omitted
when neither pool contributes. The latter is the case in the reserved skill-free episodes and at the
start of training when both pools are still empty. The task-level section is written once before the
first observation and stays in context for the episode, whereas the step-level section is rewritten after
each observation.

F.2 Internalization by Task Family
Figure 8 tracks three quantities for each of the six ALFWorld task families: the family utility of
its task-level skills, their mean mastery, and the policy’s success rate without the library. Family
utility pools the within-group utility estimates of Section 2.1 across comparisons of the family’s skills,
measuring what those skills add to an episode from that family at each step. The three quantities
evolve as the lifecycle framework anticipates, with the six families showing three distinct patterns.
Internalized families. In Pick, the policy solves 81% of validation games without skills by the
end of training, up from 66% at the start. Family utility falls from 0.09 to 0.04, and three skills are
retired. In Cool, utility peaks at 0.05 around steps 60 to 120, when mastery is highest. Five skills
are retired between steps 115 and 160, and utility returns to zero by the end.
Families that continue to require support. Clean maintains a family utility of 0.10 throughout
training, with skill-free success between 21% and 32%. Heat and Look end at 0.09 and 0.06,
respectively. Where the policy continues to fail without skills, family utility persists.
The family the library does not affect. In Pick2, the hardest family, utility remains at zero,
mastery stays at 0.33, and no skill is retired. The policy improves from 16% to 39% through
reinforcement learning alone, while the library neither improves nor degrades its performance.

   G Broader Impacts

  The components this work adds are a bookkeeping layer over an existing training pipeline, and their
  main external effect is to make an agent’s accumulated context trackable. Every estimate and every
  lifecycle transition is recorded together with the statistics that justified them, so a third party can
  replay offline why a skill was kept, retired, or removed. We regard this as the useful direction of the
  contribution, since a skill library is otherwise an opaque store of model-generated text that silently
  shapes an agent’s behavior.
  The same mechanism carries the corresponding risk. Skills are distilled by a language model
  from its own failures, so they can encode mistaken or unsafe generalizations, and a skill that is
  merely useless rather than harmful is retained as long as its measured utility is not negative. The
  evidence threshold delays every exit by design, so a harmful skill remains in the library until enough

be supplemented by checks that do not wait for statistical evidence. We also note that skill text is
derived from trajectories, so in any application where trajectories contain sensitive data, the library
inherits that sensitivity and should be handled as derived data rather than as configuration.
