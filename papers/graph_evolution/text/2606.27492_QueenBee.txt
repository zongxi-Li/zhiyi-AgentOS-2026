                                             LLM-based multi-agent systems show great potential for complex tasks through role special-
                                             ization and collaborative reasoning. However, existing automated workflow design methods
                                             primarily focus on one-off search and candidate optimization, lacking sustained accumulation
                                             and reuse of structural design experience to meet evolving coordination demands. We propose
                                             QueenBee, an experience-guided framework for multi-agent workflow evolution. QueenBee
                                             represents workflows as executable programs that generate temporal communication DAGs
                                             and maintains Structural Feedback and Transition Bank (SFT-Bank), a persistent structural
                                             memory of execution diagnoses, design hypotheses and validation outcomes, and historical
                                             programs. This accumulated experience guides subsequent workflow generation, validation,
                                             and optimization, enabling the co-evolution of collaboration programs and design experience.
                                             Experiments on Silo-Bench show that QueenBee outperforms fixed communication base-
                                             lines across team sizes and achieves the highest mean task score among recent automated
                                             multi-agent workflow optimization methods. Ablation studies further show that SFT-Bank
                                             components provide complementary performance gains, while evolution dynamics reveal a
                                             consistently improving average trend in the quality of the best collaboration structures found
                                             across independent runs as structural experience accumulates.



                                         1    Introduction
                                         LLM-based agents have shown strong abilities in reasoning, planning, and coding [6, 31, 30]. Yet
                                         real-world tasks often involve interdependent subtasks, heterogeneous tools, feedback from dynamic
                                         environments, and long-term information management [22, 7, 29]. Single agents still struggle with
                                         reliable long-term coordination and systematic verification [17, 16]. Multi-agent systems (MAS) offer
                                         an effective approach to complex tasks through role specialization, collaboration, and information
                                         exchange [7, 29, 13, 4, 27].
                                         Despite their success in collaborative reasoning and complex tasks, MAS performance depends heav-
                                         ily on workflow design. Existing frameworks often require experts to specify roles, communication
                                         topologies, execution order, and work instructions [7, 29, 13]. However, fixed structures adapt poorly
                                         to different tasks and team sizes, while poor communication may cause information loss, redundant
                                         computation, and inconsistent conclusions [41]. Manual design is therefore costly and poorly suited
                                         to changing collaboration needs.
                                         Automated MAS optimization methods mainly fall into three categories. Topology optimization
                                         methods, including GPTSwarm, G-Designer, MaAS, and MASS, optimize communication links and
                                         system architectures [45, 36, 34, 44] but lack direct program-level modeling of information transfer
                                         and integration across stages. Code and workflow search methods, including ADAS, AFlow, and
                                         A2Flow, expand the design space through program generation, execution feedback, and workflow
                                         search [8, 39, 43] but do not specifically accumulate structural modification experience from MAS

collaboration outcomes into unified, traceable design knowledge.
Prior work on experiential learning and agent self-evolution shows that systems can improve through
external memory, reusable skills, and program modifications without updating model parameters
[24, 42, 26, 28, 38, 12, 14]. However, MAS optimization requires not only better configurations but
also learning to design better coordination programs. We argue that past execution results should
become knowledge that directly guides future structural design, allowing coordination programs and
design experience to co-evolve.
We therefore represent MAS workflows as executable programs that generate temporal communication
DAGs, enabling joint optimization and inheritance of communication structures and work instructions.
Based on this representation, we propose QueenBee, an experience-guided workflow evolution
framework that uses persistent structural memory, Structural Feedback and Transition Bank (SFT-
Bank), to accumulate execution traces, design hypotheses and their validation results, and historical
programs, enabling workflows to benefit continuously from past experience.
We evaluate QueenBee on Silo-Bench and other problems across team sizes, model configurations,
and automated optimization methods, and examine its experience components through ablation
studies. Results show that QueenBee improves collaboration quality across team sizes and achieves
the highest overall task performance among the compared automated workflow optimization methods.
Our main contributions are: (1) Executable Workflow Evolution Paradigm. We represent MAS
workflows as programs generating temporal communication DAGs, enabling joint optimization
of communication structures and work instructions. (2) QueenBee and SFT-Bank. We propose
an experience-guided workflow evolution framework that continuously improves workflows by
accumulating and reusing design experience through structured memory. (3) Systematic Experimental
Evaluation. We demonstrate QueenBee’s effectiveness across team sizes and model configurations on
Silo-Bench and assess component contributions through ablation studies.


2         Related Work

2.1       Multi-Agent Communication Topology
Early LLM-based MAS relied on handcrafted collaboration: CAMEL uses role-playing [13],
MetaGPT assigns specialized development roles [7], AutoGen enables programmable agent conver-
sations [29], Multi-Agent Debate uses multi-round discussion [4], and Mixture-of-Agents employs
layered aggregation [27].


                                                                                                                         2
Later methods automate topology design. GPTSwarm jointly optimizes prompts and connections [45],
DyLAN dynamically selects agents [18], AgentPrune removes redundant edges [35], G-Designer
generates task-conditioned topologies [36], MaAS searches architectures [34], and MASS jointly
optimizes prompts and topologies [44]. However, these methods primarily optimize agent selection,
connections, or architectures, without explicitly modeling cross-round information propagation
and integration as executable coordination programs. Unlike these methods, QueenBee encodes
communication timing, information processing, and work instructions as executable coordination
programs.

2.2   Automated Workflow Optimization
ADAS generates code-based agents and archives designs [8], AFlow combines Monte Carlo Tree
Search with code modification and execution feedback [39], AgentSquare searches modular agent
configurations [23], and A2Flow introduces reusable abstractions [43]. EvoMAS evolves declarative
MAS configurations through trace-guided mutation, crossover, candidate pools, and experience
memory [9]. MAS-Architect separates topology planning from node implementation [10], while
DAAO adapts workflow depth, operators, and model routing [25]. However, these approaches lack a
unified mechanism for organizing structural modifications and their validation outcomes into reusable
experience and memory.
Unlike these methods, QueenBee evolves inheritable temporal coordination programs, linking failure
diagnoses, structural modifications, and experimental validation into reusable design knowledge.

2.3   Experience-Based Self-Improvement and Harness Evolution
External memory and skills enable improvement without parameter updates. Reflexion stores verbal
feedback [24], ExpeL extracts execution experience [42], Voyager builds executable skill libraries
[26], and Agent Workflow Memory retrieves historical workflows [28]. Promptbreeder evolves
prompts [5], STOP modifies optimization scaffolds [33], GEPA uses trajectory reflection [1], Agentic
Context Engineering evolves contextual experience [40], and Meta Context Engineering co-evolves
context skills and artifacts [32].
Program and harness evolution extend this approach. Darwin Gödel Machine evolves agent code [38],
AlphaEvolve combines code generation and evolutionary selection [19], Meta-Harness optimizes
execution frameworks [12], and Self-Harness validates modifications through failure diagnosis and
regression testing [37]. Agentic Harness Engineering emphasizes observability and reversibility [14],
Continual Harness adapts execution components online [11], and DemoEvolve uses demonstrations
under sparse feedback [2]. Harness Updating Is Not Harness Benefit distinguishes updating capability
from actual gains [15]. However, these approaches primarily improve agent capabilities or general
execution frameworks and QueenBee applies experience-driven improvement to MAS coordination
through execution diagnoses, validated design hypotheses, and inheritable programs.


3     Method

3.1   Problem Formulation
Multi-Agent Workflow. Consider n LLM-based Workers W = {W1 , . . . , Wn } jointly solving tasks
sampled from D. Each Worker Wi observes only oi , its private task information, and exchanges
information over discrete communication rounds indexed by τ . With fixed Worker parameters, an
executable Python workflow P determines (ai,τ , Ri,τ , ωi,τ ) = P (i, τ, si,τ ), where ai,τ specifies
whether to send, Ri,τ ⊆ W \ {Wi } specifies recipients, ωi,τ gives optional semantic instructions, and
si,τ is the observable state, including the round, known information sources, and pending messages.
The program also determines the termination round Tcomm , after which every Worker submits its
own final answer.
Temporal Communication DAG. Executing P on task x produces a time-unrolled DAG:
                           GP,x = Unroll(P, x) = (V, Ecomm ∪ Estate ),


                                                  3
where V = {(i, τ ) | i ∈ [n], 0 ≤ τ ≤ Tcomm }. Each node (i, τ ) represents Worker Wi at
round τ . Communication edges link a sender (i, τ ) to next-round recipients (j, τ + 1); state edges
connect consecutive states of the same Worker. All edges advance one round, ensuring acyclicity.
Topology controls information flow and timing, while semantic instructions control its processing
and expression.
Executable Representation and Objective. The executable program has two policy functions:
fcomm selects communication actions, recipients, and semantic instructions from the round, Worker
identity, and observable state; fstop determines termination. Its editable Genome contains communica-
tion phases, routing policies, and semantic work instructions. The executor unrolls these policies from
task inputs and runtime states, while Worker parameters, the underlying communication protocol,
and execution environment remain fixed. We optimize executable programs through inheritance,
modification, and combination. QueenBee repeatedly generates, executes, and evaluates candidates
to accumulate reusable experience that improves subsequent search. The design model remains fixed;
only its external experience changes. The formal design-capability objective is given in Appendix E.

3.2    SFT-Bank as Structural Memory
We introduce the Structural Feedback and Transition Bank (SFT-Bank) to provide persistent structural
memory for workflow design. SFT-Bank preserves execution diagnoses, design hypotheses and their
validation outcomes, and historical executable programs. Formally, at iteration t, the bank consists of
three components, denoted as Bt = (Dt , Lt , At ).
Diagnosis Cards Dt . Diagnosis Cards record task performance, Worker correctness distributions,
answer disagreements, information loss, and stage-level resource use, organized by propagation,
aggregation, and final-output failures.
Ledger Lt . The Ledger records each design hypothesis and validation outcome, linking parent
and candidate workflows, target tasks, intended improvements, and observed performance changes.
Attempts are labeled Confirmed, Refuted, or Inconclusive based on empirical comparisons; screened
proposals are also retained.
Program Archive At . The Program Archive stores executable source code and metadata for strong
overall programs, task-specific specialists, and successful or failed implementations.

3.3    Experience-Guided Workflow Evolution
Each iteration uses SFT-Bank to schedule exploration and retrieve historical experience. The fixed-
parameter Planner generates candidates, which the harness evaluates and uses to update SFT-Bank.

Goal Scheduling. For each of K proposals, the harness selects a goal gt,k and parent workflow Pt,k
from Bt . Most proposals address unresolved training-task failures grouped by output format and
dominant failure type. An Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) strategy prioritizes groups by remaining
improvement potential, historical quality changes, and attempt counts. Repeatedly refuted directions
receive lower priority, and each iteration favors distinct groups. The harness selects strong task-
specific parents from the Program Archive At so candidates inherit different local strengths. Other
slots periodically explore Merge, combining complementary workflows across tasks; Economize,
reducing model calls and tokens while preserving quality; and Explore, seeking alternative designs
for unresolved, underexplored tasks.

3.4    Empirical Evaluation and Experience Consolidation
Staged Empirical Evaluation. The harness first screens code and structural validity, rejecting
duplicates, constraint violations, and historically ineffective modifications. In Stage 1, it executes
each screened candidate on target training tasks and reruns its parent under identical conditions; only
candidates meeting predefined quality requirements without output-format errors advance. In Stage 2,
additional training tasks spanning diverse templates and output formats test cross-task effectiveness,
while regressions on previously strong tasks are checked. New task-specific specialists may receive
independent confirmation tests to reduce chance effects. Evaluation records task quality, execution
cost, parent-relative changes, and task-level gains and regressions.
Experience Consolidation. Updates derive from execution evidence and source-code differences,
not Planner self-assessment. Diagnosis Cards Dt receive execution statistics grouped by failure
type, excluding specific answers and ground-truth labels for safe reuse. By comparing parent and
candidate source code, the harness identifies added or removed communication stages, changes
to round scheduling, and revised work instructions. The Ledger Lt associates these changes with
task-level performance and regressions, labeling hypotheses Confirmed, Refuted, or Inconclusive;
unexecuted proposals are Screened. Confirmation requires a predefined improvement margin without
excessive regressions, while substantial target-task degradation yields refutation. The Ledger also
tracks repeatedly refuted modification directions. The Program Archive retains programs, parent-
version relationships, training evaluations, and metadata, preserving task-specific strengths for later
reuse. With feedback et,k for each candidate, the update is:

4     Experiment
4.1    Experimental Setup
We primarily evaluate QueenBee on Silo-Bench and further assess the reliability of information
propagation and aggregation using the Count-Frequency distributed statistics task (Appendix).
Benchmarks and Tasks. Silo-Bench distributes task information across Workers, requiring them to
integrate private information through communication to solve tasks beyond any individual Worker’s
capability. It comprises 30 task types, three difficulty levels, and varying computational requirements.
We use 18 development task types and 12 independent test task types.
Baselines. Fixed communication baselines include Silo-Bench’s round-robin peer-to-peer commu-
nication (P2P), Broadcast, and staged forwarding (SFS), alongside the unevolved initial workflow
P0 . Automated optimization baselines include GPTSwarm [45], G-Designer [36], MaAS [34], and
EvoMAS [9]. We also assess the role of historical experience through memory-free search and
SFT-Bank component ablations.
Model and Implementation Settings. We use GPT-5.1 and GPT-5 mini [20], DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash
[3], and gpt-oss-120b [21] models as Planners or Workers across team sizes and model combinations.

4.2    Main Result

We evaluate QueenBee against fixed communication protocols and automated workflow optimization
methods on Silo-Bench with 2, 5, and 10 Workers, with results reported in Table 1.
QueenBee outperforms fixed communication protocols. We compare QueenBee with P2P, Broad-
cast, SFS, and the initial workflow under gpt-oss-120b and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash Planner / Worker
configurations. With 5 Workers, QueenBee achieves 54.4% SR under gpt-oss and 83.3% PCS un-
der DeepSeek, surpassing the best fixed protocol, Broadcast, by 13.6 and 10.4 percentage points,
respectively. QueenBee achieves the highest task quality in both configurations, showing that jointly
optimizing communication structures and information processing improves collaboration within fixed
teams.
QueenBee outperforms existing automated workflow optimization methods. We further compare
QueenBee with Random Search, GPTSwarm, G-Designer, EvoMAS, and MaAS using gpt-oss-120b
and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, identical task splits, and a budget of 80 effective task executions. With
5 Workers, QueenBee achieves the highest scores under both models (54.4% SR under gpt-oss and
83.3% PCS under DeepSeek), exceeding the strongest baselines, EvoMAS (42.8%) and Random
Search and G-Designer (both 78.3%), by 11.6 and 5.0 percentage points, respectively.
Overall, QueenBee outperforms fixed protocols across model configurations and achieves the highest
overall task performance among automated optimization methods, demonstrating the effectiveness of
experience-guided executable workflow evolution.

4.4    Performance over Evolution
To assess QueenBee’s ability to continually improve collaboration structures, we examine workflow
performance across evolution rounds for teams of 2, 5, and 10 Workers. As Figure 2 shows, historical
workflows are evaluated retrospectively on held-out tasks after evolution, with test data excluded
from candidate generation, validation, experience updates, and selection. The trajectories thus reflect
how discovered workflows perform on unseen tasks.
Structural evolution yields sustained performance gains. As Figure 2 shows, mean PCS rises
stepwise across all three team sizes. Smaller teams saturate early, whereas larger teams continue
to improve in later rounds. Some rounds yield no improvement, while effective structures raise
performance to new plateaus. Through candidate generation, staged validation, and historical program
retention, QueenBee converts continued exploration into cumulative workflow improvements.
Larger teams gain more from evolution. With more Workers, each Worker handles a smaller
shard of the task and completes it more reliably, but the initial propagation structure integrates these
distributed results poorly, so initial PCS declines as team size increases. As evolution proceeds, this
propagation disadvantage is gradually worn away: the Planner learns from accumulated experience
how structural changes affect performance and combines effective structures, so that trial and error
becomes directed. The advantage of better per-shard execution then emerges. The 10-Worker team,
which starts from the lowest initial performance, achieves the largest gain and the highest final PCS,
reversing the initial ordering across team sizes.
Evolution gains stem from coordination optimization. Each evolution run fixes the Worker model
and task configuration, optimizing only communication structures and work instructions. Thus,
observed gains reflect improved workflow organization rather than changes in underlying model
capabilities. The improving trajectories and the reversed ordering across team sizes indicate that
QueenBee progressively discovers and retains higher-quality executable collaboration workflows
through repeated exploration, empirical validation, and reuse of historical designs.

improvements; without the Ledger Lt , it loses systematic records and use of past structural mod-

organizing execution history.
Removing the Program Archive causes the largest drop. Among single-component ablations,
w/o Archive performs worst (63.9% SR, 65.4% PCS). It removes both historical program examples
and archive-based parent selection, concentrating search on the current incumbent. This suggests
that retaining and reusing historical programs is important for experience-guided search: inheritance,
combination, and modification let the Planner exploit past designs beyond a single incumbent. Its
score slightly below w/o SFT-Bank reflects run-to-run LLM fluctuation, not a negative contribution.
Overall, full SFT-Bank outperforms all ablations. The Program Archive At and associated structural
inheritance have the greatest impact, while Diagnosis Cards and the Ledger provide additional
gains. Representative workflow analysis also reveals distinct information-processing strategies across
experience configurations.


5     Conclusion
We propose QueenBee, an experience-guided multi-agent workflow evolution framework that rep-
resents workflows as executable programs generating temporal communication DAGs. Through
persistent structural memory, SFT-Bank, QueenBee continuously accumulates execution traces,
design hypotheses and their validation results, and historical programs, enabling workflow design
to reuse past experience. This transforms execution results into knowledge guiding subsequent
structural design, allowing coordination programs and design experience to co-evolve. Experiments
on Silo-Bench show that QueenBee improves collaboration quality across team sizes and achieves the
highest overall task performance among the compared automated workflow optimization methods.


Limitations
This study has several limitations. First, QueenBee is evaluated mainly on controlled tasks such as
Silo-Bench and Count-Frequency; its generalization to open-ended real-world tasks and dynamic
environments remains unverified. Second, workflow evolution requires repeated candidate genera-


                                                     9
tion and empirical evaluation, incurring additional search costs, while performance gains in some
configurations entail higher execution costs. Furthermore, the experiments cover limited task scales,
repetitions, and model combinations, providing insufficient evidence of experience transfer across
tasks and models and no guarantee of sustained performance gains throughout evolution. Future work
will explore broader applications, cross-task experience reuse, and more efficient evolution strategies
.Finally, repeated LLM-based search may increase energy consumption, while deploying automati-
cally evolved workflows in high-stakes applications risks propagating errors without adequate human
oversight.

A.1.1    Experimental Setup
Task Definition. Count-Frequency (CF) is a controlled distributed information aggregation task
assessing information coverage and global integration in multi-agent workflows. Given an integer
array A partitioned into N disjoint private shards A1 , . . . , AN , each Worker accesses only its own
shard. Through inter-agent communication and collaboration, the system must recover the global
frequency of each integer:

where v belongs to a finite value set U. A prediction is fully correct only if every count in the
predicted frequency table is correct.
Experimental Configuration. The original CF experiments use 8 Workers, primarily with array
length |A| = 1024 and integer values 0–9. Core experiments use DeepSeek-V4-Flash for execution,
planning, and analysis, with additional exploratory runs using DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3.5, GPT-4o, and
MiMo-V2-Flash. The core evolution consists of 5 rounds, each generating 4 candidate structures and
retaining 2 for further optimization.
Baselines. We consider three fixed communication topologies: Tree progressively integrates local
counts through hierarchical reduction; Mesh–Star exchanges information through dense commu-
nication before central aggregation; and One-peer Exponential DAG–Star combines exponentially
spaced single-neighbor communication with final star aggregation. Cold-start Free DAG denotes
a freely generated communication structure without historical experience, while Self-evolved Free
DAG denotes a structure obtained through repeated generation, execution evaluation, and historical
candidate retention.

A.2    Case Study
Experimental Setup. We use 8 Subagents to process an integer array of length 1024 with values

workflows evaluated on 10 independent test instances.

and a Cold-start Graph without inherited experience. Performance is measured by RMSE between
predicted and true frequency tables, with lower values indicating more accurate aggregation. We
compare errors on paired test instances and report 95% bootstrap confidence intervals and two-sided
sign-flip test results.
Results Analysis. As Table A3 shows, QueenBee’s evolved champion achieves the lowest mean
RMSE (3.28 ± 0.84) across 10 test instances and the smallest error variation, compared with standard

decreases by 35%, with 8 wins and 2 losses across 10 instances (p = 0.020). Relative to other fixed
topologies and Cold-start Graph, errors decrease by 39%–79%, with all corresponding p-values below
0.01.

errors of all baselines. Cold-start Graph has a mean RMSE of 14.40, substantially higher than
QueenBee, indicating better aggregation by the experience-guided structure in these tests.
These results demonstrate QueenBee’s potential to discover high-quality communication structures
for precise distributed information aggregation, providing supplementary evidence of its applicability
beyond Silo-Bench.


B     Qualitative Case Study of Ablation

B.1    Comparison of Evolved Workflows
To further examine how SFT-Bank components affect workflow design, we compare the final exe-
cutable programs selected by full QueenBee and four ablated variants. All five runs start from the
same initial relay workflow but yield distinct communication structures and information-processing
instructions.
Full QueenBee adopts a Complete Snapshot strategy: Workers first send all private data to a central
node, which organizes raw information by source ID and broadcasts it, explicitly forbidding omissions,
modifications, reordering, or premature computation of the final answer. In contrast, w/o Diagnosis
and w/o Ledger delegate global computation to the central node, w/o Archive retains the initial relay
structure, and the memory-free variant lacks explicit constraints on final broadcast content. These
differences show that automated workflow search changes not only communication topology but also
information preservation and computational responsibilities.

B.2    Performance on Representative Tasks
We select three test tasks with substantial performance differences across ablations: I-10 (Standard

C     Case Study on Planner-Generated Workflows
To examine differences in structural design and information-processing rules across Planners, we
compare QueenBee champion programs evolved by DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-thinking-max and gpt-oss-
120b-thinking-high. Both runs start from the same relay workflow but use their respective Worker
models during evolution. We fix the test Worker to gpt-oss-120b and analyze Silo-Bench task III-29
(Load Balance).
Workflow Design Differences. The DeepSeek Planner replaces the initial relay chain with centralized
gathering and broadcasting, explicitly instructing Workers to label Agent IDs and preserve all data
verbatim without omissions or reordering. The central node then broadcasts a shared, complete
information snapshot. In contrast, the gpt-oss Planner retains the relay chain and adds a digest
stage. All three communication stages use the same general instruction: "Include all facts you have
received so far and your private data in your message." Explicit source labels and data-completeness
constraints are absent.
Execution Analysis. III-29 requires five Workers to aggregate 100 distributed tasks and compute
load variance, maximum, and minimum values using a prescribed greedy algorithm. In one failed
gpt-oss execution, Agent 1 omits all 10 tasks from Agent 0 when forwarding information to Agent 2.
Agent 4 then omits 66 accumulated upstream tasks during the final broadcast, sending only its own
24 tasks. Although the recorded message transmission loss is zero, none of the five Workers receives
complete data. They access only 34, 56, 68, 90, and 90 tasks, respectively, and submit five distinct
incorrect answers.
By contrast, the DeepSeek workflow broadcasts an identical snapshot containing all 100 tasks to every
Worker in all three runs. All Workers answer correctly in two runs; in the third, only one Worker
fails due to its own computation error. This contrast suggests that explicit information-completeness
constraints reduce the risk of content omission in multi-hop communication and help distinguish
information propagation errors from final computation errors.


D     Cost Analysis
We analyze QueenBee’s offline search costs, deployment inference costs, and long-term amortization,

deployable incumbent workflow and persistent SFT-Bank, enabling further search as new execution
evidence arrives. Challengers must pass a promotion gate before replacing the incumbent. Freez-
ing workflows in experiments ensures independent evaluation on unseen tasks without precluding
subsequent evolution.

D.2    Cost Breakdown
A single QueenBee evolution run consumes 5.477M tokens: approximately 4.863M for task execution
and 0.613M for 16 successful Planner calls. Task execution comprises approximately 3.817M tokens
for evolutionary evaluation and 1.048M for final validation and selection. Thus, approximately 89%
of search costs arise from task execution, primarily paired challenger–parent comparisons and final
validation, while Planner-generated structural modifications account for approximately 11%.
SFT-Bank management is not a major token cost. For comparison, the memory-free variant con-
sumes 5.550M tokens per search, while removing Diagnosis Cards or the Program Archive yields
6.507M and 7.005M, respectively. Thus, structured experience does not increase total search token
consumption in these experiments.


D.3    Cost Amortization
QueenBee’s main additional costs occur during offline evolution and do not increase per-task search
overhead after deployment. Moreover, evaluations of different candidates and training tasks within
an iteration are independent and can run in parallel to reduce wall-clock search time.
For systems repeatedly processing similar tasks, offline search costs can be amortized over deployment.
QueenBee has lower cumulative token consumption than GPTSwarm and G-Designer from the first
deployed task, and amortizes its search cost relative to search-free fixed protocols after approximately
150–505 tasks.
One evolution run costs approximately as many tokens as 88 tasks executed by the final QueenBee
workflow. Over 10,000 tasks, its amortized evolution cost is approximately 0.548k tokens per task, or
0.87% of per-task inference cost.
Thus, QueenBee’s cost advantage depends on optimization overhead, deployment efficiency, and task
volume, rather than search or inference cost alone.


D.4    Applicable Scenarios
QueenBee is particularly suited to multi-agent systems that repeatedly process similar tasks. For
example, analyses requiring periodic joint statistics, cross-checking, or auditing across departments
or institutions with distributed data can reuse evolved workflows, reducing amortized search costs.
Structural optimization is also valuable when coordination becomes a bottleneck as teams grow. In
the 10-Worker Silo-Bench experiment, QueenBee improves PCS by 49.9 percentage points over the
initial workflow, indicating its ability to mitigate information integration difficulties in larger teams.

Overall, QueenBee trades reusable offline search costs for higher-quality collaborative workflows.

E     Additional Method Details
E.1    Formal Design Objective
Rather than directly searching for high-performing workflows, QueenBee accumulates reusable
design experience through repeated candidate generation, execution, and evaluation. The design
system and its update at iteration t are defined as:
                        Mt = (Plannerθ , Bt ), Pt = Generate(Mt ; bt ),
                                Bt+1 = U (Bt , Pt , Eval(Pt )) .

Here, Plannerθ is a fixed-parameter design model, Bt is experience accumulated over t iterations, bt
is the current design budget, Pt is the candidate set, Eval denotes execution evaluation, and U updates
experience. Updating external experience rather than Planner parameters changes the information
and search guidance available for subsequent design.
We measure design capability by the quality of workflows generated under a fixed budget b:
                                 J(Mt ; b) = Ex∼Deval [Q(Pt∗ , x)] ,
where Pt∗ is generated within budget b and selected using development-stage evidence, while Q
measures its execution quality on an independent evaluation task x. Thus, J measures workflow
design capability under equal resource constraints, not the performance of an existing workflow. Our
objective is:
                                          max           J(MT ; b)
                                    HT : Cexplore (HT )≤B
where HT is a feasible exploration history comprising candidate generation, execution, and feedback;
B is the total experience accumulation budget; and MT is the design system after T updates.
Thus, we aim to improve workflow search and optimization efficiency under limited budgets through
a fixed Planner and evolving external experience, ultimately yielding better workflows.

E.2    Exploration Scheduling Details
At iteration t, the harness schedules goals and parent workflows for K proposal slots:
                                           par
                                  (gt,k , Pt,k ) = Schedule(Bt , k).

Here, k denotes the proposal slot. Most proposals target quality improvements, while the remainder
rotate among structural merging, cost optimization, and open exploration.
For targeted improvements, the harness groups unresolved training tasks by output format and
dominant failure type. An Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) strategy prioritizes groups based on
remaining improvement potential, historical quality changes, and exploration counts. Directions
repeatedly refuted in the Ledger Lt receive lower priority, while each iteration favors coverage of
distinct groups.
For each selected goal, the harness chooses a strong task-specific program from the Program Archive
At as the parent, allowing candidates to inherit different local strengths and explore multiple direc-
tions.
The harness also periodically schedules three exploration modes: Merge combines workflows
with complementary strengths across training tasks; Economize reduces model calls and token
consumption while preserving quality; and Explore targets unresolved, underexplored tasks to
discover alternatives to existing successful designs.


                                                   19
E.3    Experience Retrieval Details
The harness constructs each candidate’s design context using the three SFT-Bank components.
From Diagnosis Cards Dt , it categorizes problematic training tasks by output format and observed
failure cause, then retrieves the parent’s task-specific diagnoses.
From the Ledger Lt , it aggregates validation outcomes and observed performance changes by failure
type and modification mechanism, extracting recently supported hypotheses and repeatedly ineffective
modifications.
From the Program Archive At , it prioritizes successful programs that addressed similar failures
and corresponding failure cases, providing complementary programs when combining workflows.
Retrieved examples are presented as editable code snippets, allowing the Planner to reference concrete
communication structures and execution strategies.
The Planner also receives the parent’s editable Python code (Genome), its task-level quality and cost
records, the current exploration goal, and fixed execution-interface constraints. The resulting design
hypothesis specifies the target problem, modification mechanism, and predicted improvement, while
the Genome provides its Python implementation.


E.4    Validation and Experience Consolidation Details
Candidates first undergo code and execution-structure validity checks. Duplicate programs, execution-
constraint violations, and historically ineffective modifications are rejected before task testing.
In Stage 1, the harness executes candidates on designated target training tasks and reruns their
parent workflows under identical conditions. Only candidates satisfying predefined performance
requirements without output-format errors advance.
In Stage 2, promising candidates are evaluated on additional training tasks covering diverse templates
and output formats. Regression tests check whether previously strong tasks deteriorate. Newly dis-
covered task-specific specialists may undergo independent confirmation tests to reduce the influence
of incidental execution outcomes.
After evaluation, the harness derives experience from actual execution results and code changes rather
than Planner self-assessment.
Diagnosis Cards retain structured execution statistics by predefined failure type but exclude specific
answers and ground-truth labels for safe reuse.
The Ledger identifies added or removed communication stages, round-scheduling adjustments, and
changes to work instructions through parent–candidate source comparisons. Based on target-task
performance and regression tests, hypotheses are classified as Confirmed, Refuted, or Inconclusive;
unexecuted proposals are marked Screened. Confirmation requires a predefined improvement margin
without exceeding the allowed regression threshold, while substantial target-task degradation leads to
refutation.
The harness links modification mechanisms, validation outcomes, and observed performance changes,
tracks historical performance across failure-type–modification combinations, and flags modifications
repeatedly refuted in independent tests.
The Program Archive retains new programs, parent-version relationships, training evaluations, and
metadata, maintaining historical candidates according to task performance. Screened or insufficiently
supported candidates may also leave records; experience updates do not require candidates to
outperform existing workflows.


F     Focused Validation and Final Selection
When exploration ends and a final workflow is required, the harness stops generating candidates,
selects historically strong programs from the Program Archive, and determines the final workflow
through independent validation.

To prevent programs with chance high scores on a few tasks from dominating, candidate screening
considers both task coverage and overall quality improvement. Specifically, the harness evaluates
historical candidates using a shrunk training score relative to the initial workflow P0 :

                           SeT (P ) =                  S P (u) − S P0 (u) ,
                                      |UP | + 2

where UP is the set of tasks with training evaluations for both the candidate and initial workflows,
and S P (u) is the mean execution quality of program P on task u. The denominator’s smoothing
term penalizes candidates evaluated on too few tasks, preventing locally strong programs from being
prematurely ranked as overall best. The harness further requires sufficient training-task coverage and
excludes programs showing substantial regression on previously stable tasks, yielding a representative
set of high-performing candidates.
The harness then repeatedly evaluates selected programs on identical independent validation tasks,
ranks them by mean quality across tasks, and favors lower execution cost when quality is comparable.
Top-ranked candidates undergo further regression validation to detect substantial degradation on
previously stable tasks. Independent validation results are used solely for final selection and are not
fed back to the Planner for further structural modifications, preventing validation information from
influencing earlier experience accumulation.
Finally, the harness selects an independently validated executable workflow P ∗ . Thus, broad explo-
ration discovers and accumulates diverse communication and collaboration strategies, while focused
validation selects a strong overall workflow from historical candidates, completing the process from
continual experience accumulation to final workflow selection.
